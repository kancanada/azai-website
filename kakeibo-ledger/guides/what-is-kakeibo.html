What is kakeibo? How to use the Japanese budgeting method

Updated 15 November 2026 · 8 min read
Short answerKakeibo (家計簿, “household ledger”) is a Japanese way of budgeting by writing every expense down yourself. The household account book it's named after was devised in 1904 by Hani Motoko, a journalist and magazine publisher. As it's usually taught in English today, you start each month by noting your income, fixed costs and savings goal; you record each expense in one of four categories (needs, wants, culture, unexpected); and at month end you answer four questions: how much you had, how much you wanted to save, how much you spent, and how you can improve.

We make Kakeibo Ledger, a kakeibo budget journal for Android that we mention near the end. Facts about the history of kakeibo and about other books and apps come from the sources linked below.

Most budgeting systems try to take effort out of tracking money. Kakeibo does the opposite. It asks you to write each purchase down by hand, sort it into a small number of boxes, and sit with the totals at the end of the month. The small amount of effort is the point: you can't write “$14 lunch” for the fourth time in a week without noticing.

This guide covers where the method comes from, what it looks like in practice, and how to run your first month, whether you use a notebook, a spreadsheet or an app.

Where kakeibo comes from

The word kakeibo (家計簿) simply means a household account book. The version people mean when they talk about “the kakeibo method” goes back to Hani Motoko (1873–1957). Japan's National Diet Library describes her as the first woman journalist in Japan. With her husband, Hani Yoshikazu, she published the magazine Katei no Tomo, which later became Fujin no Tomo (“Women's Friend”), and in 1921 she co-founded the Jiyu Gakuen school.

Fujin no Tomo-sha, the publisher she founded, says she devised the “Hani Motoko household account book” in 1904, and it still publishes a version of it every year. That makes it one of the longest-running personal finance tools anywhere, older than the credit card or the spreadsheet.

The method reached English-speaking readers much later, mainly through books such as Kakeibo: The Japanese Art of Budgeting & Saving Money, published by Penguin in 2017. Penguin's description sums it up as setting a savings goal each month, noting what you spend, and reflecting on the month before.

One honest caveat: the tidy “four categories and four questions” version is how kakeibo is usually taught in English-language books and apps. Japanese household ledgers, including the one Hani Motoko designed, tend to use more detailed expense headings and a yearly budget. The spirit is the same; the paperwork is simpler.

The method in one page

Kakeibo has three parts, and they follow the month.

1. Plan, on the first day of the month

Write down four numbers:

  • Income: what will actually land in your account this month, after tax.
  • Fixed costs: the bills that come out no matter what you do: rent or mortgage, insurance, phone, internet, loan payments, subscriptions you're keeping.
  • Savings goal: what you want to set aside, decided now, not with whatever is left.
  • Spending money: income minus fixed costs minus savings. This is the money the rest of the month is about.

2. Write, every day you spend

Each purchase goes in the ledger with the date, a few words about what it was, and the amount, in one of four categories:

  • Needs: groceries, transport, medicine, the basics you'd pay for in any month.
  • Wants: eating out, shopping, takeaway coffee, things that are nice but optional.
  • Culture: books, courses, museums, concerts, hobbies that feed your mind.
  • Unexpected: repairs, a vet bill, a gift you forgot about, anything no plan saw coming.

Our guide to the four kakeibo categories has a long list of examples and the grey areas people argue about.

3. Reflect, at the end of the month

Answer four questions, in writing:

  1. How much did you have?
  2. How much did you want to save?
  3. How much did you spend?
  4. How can you improve?

The first three are arithmetic. The fourth is the one that changes things, and it works best as one specific change for next month rather than a general resolution. There's a longer list of follow-up questions in our monthly reflection guide.

A worked example

Here's a made-up but realistic month for one person.

LineAmountNotes
Take-home income$3,800Two paycheques
Fixed costs$2,050Rent $1,600, phone $65, internet $70, insurance $120, transit pass $135, streaming $60
Savings goal$400Moved to savings on payday
Spending money$1,350$3,800 − $2,050 − $400

A first split of that $1,350 might be $700 for needs, $350 for wants, $100 for culture and $200 for unexpected. Over the month, the ledger fills up: groceries and pharmacy runs under needs, two dinners out and a jacket under wants, a used book and a pottery class under culture, a cracked phone screen under unexpected.

At month end the totals read: needs $640, wants $520, culture $85, unexpected $180. Total spent: $1,425, which is $75 more than planned. Savings still happened because they came out first, but the overspend had to come from somewhere (in this case, the chequing account buffer).

The reflection might say: “Wants went $170 over, almost all of it eating out on Fridays after work. Next month: Friday dinner is fine, but one a fortnight, not every week.” That's a kakeibo month done properly. Nothing dramatic, one clear lesson, and a specific change.

How to start your first month

  1. Pick your tool. A small notebook you carry, a printed sheet, a spreadsheet or an app. We have a free kakeibo template you can print or copy. The best tool is the one you'll have with you when you pay.
  2. Do the plan with real numbers. Pull up last month's bank and card statements to get your fixed costs right. Guessing low on fixed costs is the most common way a first kakeibo month goes wrong.
  3. Save first. If you can, move the savings amount on payday. If you can't yet, set a small goal you'll actually hit. A $50 goal you meet teaches more than a $500 goal you abandon.
  4. Write things down the same day. Most people who give up on tracking do so because they fall a week behind and can't reconstruct it. Same-day entries take seconds.
  5. Check in once a week. A five-minute look on Sunday shows whether one category is running hot while there's still time to adjust.
  6. Close the month. Do the four questions within a few days of month end, while you still remember why the numbers look the way they do.

Expect the first month to be messy. You'll misjudge a category or two and forget a few coffees. That's normal. The second month's plan will be far more accurate because it's built on the first month's reality.

Why writing it down works

Kakeibo's main idea is that awareness changes behaviour. When a bank app sorts your spending automatically, you see a pie chart once in a while. When you write each purchase yourself, you meet every one of them. Many people find that simply knowing they'll have to record a purchase makes them pause before it.

It isn't only a Japanese idea, either. Canada's Financial Consumer Agency suggests a similar exercise before you build a budget: track everything you buy for a month or two, including small purchases, and sort it into needs and wants. Kakeibo turns that one-off exercise into a monthly habit with a reflection at the end.

We don't want to oversell it. Writing things down won't raise your income or cut your rent, and it takes a few minutes most days. What it reliably gives you is an accurate picture, which is the starting point for every other money decision.

Who kakeibo suits, and who it doesn't

It suits you if you don't really know where your money goes, you want to spend more deliberately rather than just less, you dislike linking your bank to apps, or you've tried detailed budgeting apps and drowned in categories.

It may not suit you if you need to manage several accounts, credit cards and debts together, you share finances with a partner who wants to see everything live, or you know you won't record things by hand. In those cases a full budgeting app such as YNAB or Goodbudget may serve you better; we compare them in kakeibo vs YNAB.

Irregular income isn't a dealbreaker. Plan the month on a conservative income figure, and treat anything above it as a bonus to save or spend on purpose.

Questions

Who invented kakeibo?

The household account book behind the kakeibo method was devised in 1904 by Hani Motoko, a Japanese journalist and the founder of the publisher Fujin no Tomo-sha, which still publishes a version of it.

What are the four kakeibo questions?

How much money do you have? How much would you like to save? How much are you spending? How can you improve? The first three come from your plan and your records; the fourth is a written reflection.

What are the four kakeibo categories?

Needs, wants, culture and unexpected. Needs are essentials like groceries and transport, wants are optional purchases like eating out, culture covers books, courses and hobbies, and unexpected covers repairs, medical costs and other surprises.

Do you have to write kakeibo by hand?

No. Traditional kakeibo is a paper book, but the method works in a spreadsheet or an app. What matters is that you record each expense yourself rather than importing it automatically.

Is kakeibo good for beginners?

Yes. It has only four categories and a short monthly routine, so it is one of the easier budgeting methods to start. Expect the first month to be rough; the second month's plan is built on real numbers.
