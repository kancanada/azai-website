The four kakeibo categories, with examples

Updated 15 November 2026 · 7 min read
Short answerKakeibo sorts day-to-day spending into four boxes. Needs are things you'd pay for in any month: groceries, transport, medicine, basic toiletries. Wants are optional: eating out, takeaway coffee, clothes you don't strictly need, entertainment. Culture is spending that grows your mind: books, courses, museums, concerts, hobbies. Unexpected is what no plan saw coming: repairs, medical bills, a last-minute gift. Fixed costs like rent usually sit in the monthly plan, not in the four boxes.

We make Kakeibo Ledger, a kakeibo budget journal for Android that we mention near the end. Facts about the history of kakeibo and about other books and apps come from the sources linked below.

The four categories are what make kakeibo feel different from other budgets. Most budgeting apps start you with twenty or thirty categories, and people end up spending more time sorting than noticing. Kakeibo gives you four, and asks a simpler question about each purchase: what kind of spending was this?

This guide explains each category, lists everyday examples, and deals with the grey areas that trip people up. If you're new to the method, start with what is kakeibo.

You'll see other names for the same idea. Some books and apps call them survival, optional, culture and extra. The labels matter less than using them the same way every month.

Needs

Needs are the variable essentials: the things that keep your household running that aren't a fixed bill. If you cut them to zero, something would go wrong.

  • Groceries and household supplies (cleaning products, toilet paper)
  • Fuel, parking, transit fares not covered by a pass
  • Prescriptions and over-the-counter medicine
  • Basic toiletries and hygiene
  • Children's essentials: nappies, school supplies, clothes they've outgrown
  • Work costs you can't avoid, such as safety boots or a required uniform
  • Pet food and routine pet care

The trap with needs is that they quietly absorb wants. A grocery run can include $40 of snacks and wine; the “need” is the food, not every item in the basket. Canada's Financial Consumer Agency makes a useful point here: needs differ from person to person, and change over time. A car can be a need in a rural town and a want in a city with good transit. Decide what counts as a need for you, and be consistent.

Wants

Wants are spending you choose for pleasure or convenience. There's nothing wrong with them; kakeibo isn't about cutting all wants, it's about seeing them clearly.

  • Restaurants, takeaway and food delivery
  • Coffee, snacks and drinks out
  • Clothes, shoes and accessories beyond what you need
  • Gadgets, games, apps and in-app purchases
  • Bars, nights out, cinema tickets for a blockbuster
  • Upgrades: the premium version of something you'd buy anyway
  • Ride-shares you took because it was easier, not because you had to

For most people, wants is the box that goes over. That's useful information, not a failure. The month-end question is whether the wants were worth it.

Culture

Culture is the category that surprises people, and it's the one that gives kakeibo its character. It's spending on things that make you more knowledgeable, more skilled or more connected to the arts: money spent on who you're becoming.

  • Books, e-books, audiobooks, magazines and newspapers
  • Courses, workshops, language classes, tutoring
  • Museum, gallery, theatre and concert tickets
  • Music lessons, instrument strings, art supplies
  • Hobby materials: yarn, seeds, woodworking tools
  • Entry fees for a talk, a heritage site or a festival

Giving culture its own box does two things. It stops valuable spending from being lumped in with impulse buys, and it makes you notice if you spent nothing on it all month. Some people set a small culture budget on purpose, as a reminder to use it.

Unexpected

Unexpected is for costs that weren't on your radar when you made the month's plan. The test is timing, not size.

  • Car, bike or appliance repairs
  • Dental work, physio, a vet visit
  • Replacing something broken or lost
  • A wedding or birthday gift you forgot about
  • A parking ticket or late fee
  • Last-minute travel for a family reason

If the same “unexpected” cost shows up every few months (car maintenance, annual subscriptions, birthday gifts), it isn't really unexpected. Put a monthly amount for it in your plan instead, and you'll be less surprised next time.

The grey areas

These come up in almost every first kakeibo month. There's no single right answer, but here's a sensible default for each.

Three rules for deciding quickly

  1. Use the tight-month test. If money were very tight this month, would you still buy it? Yes means needs; no means wants or culture.
  2. Ask whether it grows you. If a want also teaches you something or connects you to the arts, culture is the better home.
  3. Consistency beats accuracy. It matters less whether your gym goes in wants or culture than that it goes in the same box every month. Otherwise month-to-month comparisons stop meaning anything.

And if you're stuck at the till, pick one and move on. You can change it at the weekly check-in.

How to split your budget across the four boxes

Kakeibo doesn't prescribe fixed percentages. A reasonable first guess for your spending money (what's left after fixed costs and savings) is about half for needs, a quarter for wants, a tenth for culture and the rest for unexpected. Kakeibo Ledger suggests exactly that starting split: 50%, 25%, 10% and 15%.

Your real numbers will differ. If you live in an expensive city, needs may take more; if you have a car that's getting old, unexpected may need more room. After one month you'll have actual totals, and the second month's split should be built on those, not on a rule of thumb. If you're curious how this compares with a percentage-based rule, see kakeibo vs the 50/30/20 rule.

Why only four?

Because more categories means more decisions and fewer insights. With thirty categories, the month-end view is a long list where nothing stands out. With four, the picture is immediate: wants went over, culture was empty, unexpected was quiet. That's enough to choose one thing to change.

If you want detail inside a box, use the note on each entry (“lunch with team”, “cat food”) rather than more categories. The notes are there when you reflect, without making each entry a sorting exercise.

Using the four boxes in an app

In Kakeibo Ledger, which we make, the four categories are fixed: you can't add, rename or delete them, on purpose. Questions

What are the four categories in kakeibo?

Needs, wants, culture and unexpected. Some books call them survival, optional, culture and extra. Fixed costs such as rent and insurance are usually handled in the monthly plan rather than in the four categories.

What goes in the culture category in kakeibo?

Spending that feeds your mind or a skill: books, magazines, courses, workshops, museum and concert tickets, music lessons and hobby supplies.

Is rent a need in kakeibo?

Rent is a need, but in kakeibo it is usually treated as a fixed cost in the monthly plan, taken out before you budget the four categories. The ledger is for variable, day-to-day spending.

Where do gifts go in kakeibo?

Planned gifts usually go in wants, and forgotten or last-minute gifts in unexpected. If gifts come up every year, set aside a monthly amount in your plan for them.

Can I add more categories to kakeibo?

You can, but the method works best with four. If you want more detail, write a short note on each entry instead of adding categories, so month-end totals stay easy to read.
