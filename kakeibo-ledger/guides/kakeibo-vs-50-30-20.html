Kakeibo vs the 50/30/20 rule: which budget fits you?

Updated 15 November 2026 · 6 min read
Short answerThe 50/30/20 rule, popularized by Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi in All Your Worth (2005), is a target: about 50% of after-tax income on needs, 30% on wants and 20% on savings and debt. Kakeibo is a routine: plan the month with savings taken out first, write every expense in four categories, and reflect at month end. 50/30/20 tells you roughly where your money should go; kakeibo shows you where it actually went. Many people use both: 50/30/20 to set the savings goal, kakeibo to track the month.

We make Kakeibo Ledger, a kakeibo budget journal for Android that we mention near the end. Facts about the history of kakeibo and about other books and apps come from the sources linked below.

“Kakeibo or 50/30/20?” sounds like a choice between two budgets, but they answer different questions. One is a set of proportions. The other is a habit of writing things down and looking back. Once you see that, it's easier to decide which you need, or whether you want both.

The 50/30/20 rule in brief

The 50/30/20 rule comes from All Your Worth by Elizabeth Warren and her daughter Amelia Warren Tyagi, first published in 2005. The idea is to divide your after-tax income into three parts:

  • 50% must-haves: the costs you're committed to: housing, utilities, insurance, minimum loan payments, groceries, transport to work.
  • 30% wants: everything else you spend on: eating out, holidays, hobbies, shopping.
  • 20% savings: emergency savings, retirement, and paying down debt beyond the minimums.

Its strength is simplicity. You don't need to track every coffee; you need to check that the big blocks are roughly in proportion. If must-haves take 65% of your income, the rule tells you that something structural (rent, a car payment) is squeezing everything else.

Kakeibo in brief

Kakeibo comes from the household account book Hani Motoko devised in Japan in 1904. As it's usually practised today:

  • At the start of the month you note income, fixed costs and a savings goal, and work out your spending money.
  • During the month you write down every expense in one of four categories: needs, wants, culture and unexpected.
  • At month end you answer four questions: how much you had, how much you wanted to save, how much you spent, and how you can improve.

Kakeibo has no official percentages. It's about awareness and steady adjustment, month after month. The full method is in what is kakeibo.

The same month, both ways

Take a household with $5,000 a month after tax.

With 50/30/20

The targets are $2,500 for must-haves, $1,500 for wants and $1,000 for savings. Looking at last month's statements, must-haves came to $2,900 (rent $1,900, utilities and insurance $400, groceries $450, transit $150), wants to $1,600, and savings to $500. The verdict: must-haves are 58% of income, and savings are half the target. The rule points at a big decision: housing is high, so either income rises, rent falls, or wants have to give way to savings.

With kakeibo

The plan: income $5,000, fixed costs $2,450 (rent, utilities, insurance, transit), savings goal $700. Spending money: $1,850. During the month every purchase is written down. At month end: needs (mainly groceries) $480, wants $1,080, culture $60, unexpected $310. Total $1,930, $80 over plan. The notes show that $420 of wants was food delivery on weeknights.

The reflection: “Delivery on work nights is the leak. Next month: batch cook on Sundays, delivery once a week.” That's a specific change the 50/30/20 view could never show you, because it doesn't look at individual purchases.

Notice that each method found something real. 50/30/20 found the structural problem (rent and a low savings rate). Kakeibo found the behavioural one (delivery). Neither found both.

Which one should you use?

Choose 50/30/20 if you want a quick health check, you don't want to track daily spending, and your problem is more about the big picture than the details. It's also a good starting point if you've never budgeted and want a benchmark.

Choose kakeibo if you earn enough on paper but money still disappears, you want to spend more mindfully, or you've tried rule-of-thumb budgets and found they didn't change anything day to day.

Be careful with 50/30/20 if you live somewhere with high housing costs. Rent alone can take most of the 50% in many cities, and the rule can feel like a verdict you can't act on this month. Treat it as a direction to move in over time, not a pass-or-fail test.

Be careful with kakeibo if you know you won't record things daily. A kakeibo ledger with half the purchases missing is worse than a rough 50/30/20 check, because it gives you false confidence.

How to combine them

The two methods fit together well, because 50/30/20 sets the destination and kakeibo handles the journey.

  1. Use 50/30/20 once a year (or when your income changes) as a health check. Are must-haves near half? Is saving near a fifth? If not, note the gap.
  2. Set your kakeibo savings goal from it. If 20% isn't realistic yet, pick a step towards it, say 10%, and make that the savings line in every monthly plan.
  3. Map the categories. Kakeibo's fixed costs plus needs roughly equal 50/30/20's must-haves. Wants plus culture roughly equal its wants. Unexpected splits between the two depending on what it was.
  4. Run kakeibo month to month to find the specific changes that move you towards the target.

A note on the mapping: 50/30/20 counts extra debt repayment as savings. In kakeibo, put extra debt payments in the savings line of your plan, not in the four boxes, so the comparison stays fair.

Other methods worth knowing

If neither fits, two other approaches are popular. Zero-based budgeting, used by YNAB, gives every dollar a job before you spend it, across as many categories as you like. Envelope budgeting, used by Goodbudget and by people who literally use cash envelopes, sets a fixed amount per category and stops when the envelope is empty. Kakeibo is lighter than both: fewer categories, no account balances, and more emphasis on reflection. See kakeibo vs YNAB for that comparison.

Questions

Who created the 50/30/20 rule?

It was popularized by Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi in their book All Your Worth, first published in 2005. It suggests about 50% of after-tax income for must-haves, 30% for wants and 20% for savings and debt repayment.

Is kakeibo better than the 50/30/20 rule?

Neither is better in general. 50/30/20 is a quick target for how income should be split; kakeibo is a monthly habit that shows where money actually went. Kakeibo takes more effort but finds specific changes; 50/30/20 is faster but less detailed.

Can I use kakeibo and 50/30/20 together?

Yes. Use 50/30/20 as a yearly health check and to choose your savings goal, then run kakeibo each month to track spending and find what to change.

Does kakeibo have percentages?

No fixed ones. Kakeibo asks you to set your own savings goal and plan for the month. A common starting split of spending money is about 50% needs, 25% wants, 10% culture and 15% unexpected, adjusted after the first month.

Is 50/30/20 realistic with high rent?

Often not straight away. In high-cost cities housing alone can take most of the 50%. Treat the rule as a direction to move in over time rather than a test you must pass this month.
