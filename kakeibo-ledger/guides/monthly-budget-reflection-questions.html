Monthly budget reflection: questions to ask, with a template

Updated 15 November 2026 · 6 min read
Short answerStart with kakeibo's four questions: How much did I have? How much did I want to save? How much did I spend? How can I improve? Then ask a few follow-ups: which category went over and why, what spending I'd happily repeat, what I regret, and what's coming next month. Finish with one specific change, written where you'll see it when you plan next month. Twenty minutes is enough.

Tracking spending tells you what happened. Reflection is where you decide what to do about it, and it's the step most budgets skip. Kakeibo, the Japanese household ledger method, builds it into every month: you don't start a new month until you've looked honestly at the last one.

This guide gives you a simple structure for that monthly review: the four core questions, a set of follow-ups to pick from, an example, and a template to copy.

Before you start

Set aside twenty minutes in the first few days of the new month, while the old one is still fresh. You'll need:

  • Your plan for the month (income, fixed costs, savings goal, spending budget).
  • Your spending totals by category, from your ledger, spreadsheet or app.
  • Your bank and card statements, to check that the totals are complete.
  • Last month's reflection, if you have one, to see whether last month's change worked.

A quiet table and a cup of tea help more than you'd think. This isn't an audit; it's a conversation with yourself.

The four core questions

1. How much did I have?

Your income for the month, minus fixed costs. This is the money you actually had choices about. If income was different from what you planned (overtime, a late invoice, a bonus), note it.

2. How much did I want to save?

The savings goal you set at the start. Write it down even if you missed it; the gap is information.

3. How much did I spend?

Your total spending across all categories. Then compare: did what you had, minus what you spent, reach your savings goal?

4. How can I improve?

The question that matters. Answer it last, after the follow-ups below, and make the answer one specific, doable change, not a resolution. “Spend less on food” is a resolution. “Bring lunch Monday to Thursday” is a change.

Follow-up questions to choose from

You don't need all of these every month. Pick the three or four that fit what happened.

About the numbers

  • Which category went furthest over its budget? What were the three biggest items in it?
  • Which category came in under? Was that a good choice or just a quiet month?
  • Were there any unexpected costs? Were they truly unexpected, or something that comes round every year?
  • How did my total compare with last month, and with the month before?

About how it felt

  • Which purchase am I gladdest I made?
  • Which purchase would I undo if I could?
  • When did I spend without thinking? (Time of day, mood, place, people.)
  • Did I spend anything on culture, the things that feed my mind? Would I like to?

About next month

  • What's coming up that will cost money: birthdays, renewals, trips, school costs?
  • Does my plan need to change: a new fixed cost, a different savings goal, a different split between categories?
  • Did last month's change work? Keep it, adjust it, or drop it?

Notice that the change is small, specific and tied to a pattern the numbers revealed (an impulse buy on a bad day). That's far more likely to stick than “spend less on wants”.

Making the reflection count

  • Write one change, not five. Five changes means none of them get done.
  • Put it where you'll see it. At the top of next month's plan, in your phone's lock-screen note, on the fridge.
  • Be kind. The reflection is for learning, not punishment. A month where you overspent but understood why is a good month for your budget.
  • Look back quarterly. Every three months, read your last three reflections together. Patterns that are invisible month to month (always over in wants around payday, always surprised by the same bill) become obvious.
  • Do a year in review. Once a year, look at twelve months of totals against your plans. It's the best moment to reset your savings goal and your split between categories.

If you share money with someone

Do the four questions together, but let each person answer the follow-ups for their own spending first. Then agree on one shared change. Keep it to twenty minutes and keep it about the numbers, not about blame; the numbers are usually more persuasive than an argument.

Questions

What questions should I ask in a monthly budget review?

Start with kakeibo's four: how much did I have, how much did I want to save, how much did I spend, and how can I improve. Then ask which category went over and why, which purchases you're glad of or regret, and what's coming next month.

How long should a monthly budget review take?

About 15 to 30 minutes, ideally in the first few days of the new month while you still remember why the numbers look the way they do.

What are the four kakeibo questions?

How much money do you have? How much would you like to save? How much are you spending? How can you improve?

How do I turn a budget review into real change?

Pick one specific, doable change rather than several general resolutions, write it at the top of next month's plan, and check at the next review whether it worked.

Should I also do a yearly budget review?

Yes. Once a year, look at twelve months of totals against your plans. It's the best time to reset your savings goal and how you split your spending between categories.
