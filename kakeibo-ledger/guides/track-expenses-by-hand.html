How to track expenses by hand (and keep doing it)

Updated 15 November 2026 · 6 min read
Short answerRecord every purchase the same day with three things: the amount, a category and a few words about what it was. Keep the ledger wherever you pay (a pocket notebook or your phone), use a handful of categories rather than dozens, check your bank and card statements once a week for anything you missed, and total it up at month end. Fixed bills can be noted once in a monthly plan rather than entered every time.

Tracking expenses by hand has a reputation for being tedious, and most people who try it stop within a few weeks. That's rarely because it takes too long. It's because the system was too complicated, or because they fell behind and couldn't catch up. This guide is about a simple routine that survives a busy month.

Canada's Financial Consumer Agency suggests tracking everything you buy for a month or two before you make a budget, small purchases included. That's a good place to start even if you never do anything fancier.

Why track by hand at all?

Your bank app already lists your transactions, so why write them again? Three reasons:

  • You notice. Seeing a list scroll by isn't the same as writing “$6.50, coffee” for the fifth time this week. The small act of recording is what changes behaviour. That's the core idea of kakeibo, the Japanese household ledger.
  • You categorize honestly. Automatic categories can't tell groceries from a bottle of wine on the same receipt. You can.
  • You see everything in one place. Cash, several cards, e-transfers and a partner's card end up on one page instead of five apps.

What to record

For each purchase, three fields are enough:

  1. Amount. What you actually paid, including tax and tip.
  2. Category. One of a small fixed set. Kakeibo uses four: needs, wants, culture and unexpected. If you prefer your own, keep it under eight.
  3. Note. A few words: “groceries”, “lunch with Sam”, “bike tube”. This is what makes the month-end review useful.

The date is usually implied by where you write it. Resist adding more fields like payment method, merchant or location. Every extra field makes each entry slower and the habit more fragile.

A routine that survives a busy month

Daily: record within the day

Write each purchase as you pay, or at least before bed. The test is whether you can remember it without looking anything up. If you can't, you've waited too long. Tie it to something you already do: when you put your card away, when you plug your phone in at night.

Weekly: reconcile and look

Once a week, ideally the same day, spend ten minutes on two things:

  • Check your statements. Open your bank and card apps and scan the week. Anything you forgot (an auto-renewal, a tap at the bus, a refund) goes in now. Manual tracking doesn't mean ignoring your bank; it means your bank is the backup, not the source.
  • Compare with the plan. Add up each category. If one is running hot, you still have three weeks to adjust.

Monthly: total and reflect

At month end, total each category, compare with what you planned, and write down one thing to change. Our monthly reflection questions make this a fifteen-minute job.

Tricky cases

Credit cards. Record the purchase when you make it, not when you pay the card bill. Otherwise you count everything twice, or a month late. The card payment itself isn't new spending.

Cash. Withdrawing cash isn't spending; spending it is. Either record each cash purchase, or, if that's impractical, record the withdrawal once as “cash, wants” (or whichever category it mostly goes to) and accept the rough edge.

Refunds and returns. Enter them as a negative amount in the same category as the original purchase, so the category total stays honest.

Mixed receipts. Split them. A $92 supermarket shop with $18 of wine is $74 needs and $18 wants. Two quick entries.

Shared costs. Record only your share. If you paid $80 for dinner and a friend sent you $40, record $40.

Fixed bills. Rent, insurance, phone and subscriptions don't need to be entered every month in the daily ledger. Put them in a monthly plan once and leave the daily ledger for spending you can actually change. That's how kakeibo handles them.

Annual costs. A yearly subscription or car registration lands in one month and distorts it. Either note it as unexpected and move on, or set aside a twelfth of it every month in your plan.

What to do when you fall behind

You will miss days. Here's the recovery:

  1. Don't try to reconstruct cash purchases you can't remember. Let them go.
  2. Pull card and bank transactions for the missing days from your statements and enter them in one sitting.
  3. Start again from today. A month with a three-day gap is still far more informative than no record at all.

The most common way to quit is deciding the month is “ruined” after a gap. It isn't. Totals that are 95% complete still show you where the money went.

Mistakes that make people quit

  • Too many categories. Twenty categories means twenty decisions a day. Four to eight is plenty.
  • Tracking to the cent across five fields. Speed matters more than precision. Round if you like.
  • Never looking at the totals. Tracking without reviewing is just data entry. The weekly look is what makes it worthwhile.
  • Judging yourself. The point is to see clearly, not to feel bad. An honest ledger with overspending in it is a success.

How long until it's a habit?

Most people find the daily entry becomes automatic after a few weeks, and the first full month is the hardest. Plan to do at least two full months before deciding whether it's for you: the first month shows you what you spend, and the second shows whether the changes you chose actually worked.

Questions

What is the easiest way to track expenses manually?

Record each purchase the same day with just the amount, a category from a short list, and a few words about it. Keep the record wherever you pay, check your statements weekly for anything missed, and total it up at month end.

Should I track expenses daily or weekly?

Record daily, review weekly. Same-day entries are quick and accurate; a weekly ten-minute check against your bank and card statements catches anything you forgot and shows which categories are running hot.

How do I track credit card spending by hand?

Record each purchase when you make it, in its spending category. When you pay the card bill, don't record it again, because the spending is already counted.

How many expense categories should I use?

Fewer than you think. Kakeibo uses four: needs, wants, culture and unexpected. Most people do well with four to eight; more makes each entry slower and the month-end picture harder to read.

What if I forget to track expenses for a few days?

Fill in card and bank transactions from your statements, let go of small cash purchases you can't remember, and start again from today. A month that is mostly complete is still very useful.
