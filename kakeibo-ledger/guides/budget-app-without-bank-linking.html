Budget apps that don't link to your bank (Android, 2026)

Updated 15 November 2026 · 6 min read
Short answerSeveral good budget apps work without a bank connection. On Android: Kakeibo Ledger (kakeibo method, no internet permission, free with a one-time Pro), Cashew (free, open source, data on your device), Goodbudget (envelopes; free plan with manual entry, data synced through its servers), Money Manager (detailed ledger, ads in the free version) and YNAB with unlinked accounts (subscription). The trade-off is the same for all of them: you enter spending yourself. To judge privacy, check the Data safety label, whether an account is required, and whether the app uses the internet at all.

We make Kakeibo Ledger, one of the apps compared here. We haven't tested every app below; details come from each app's own Google Play listing and website on 15 November 2026, linked under Sources. Data safety labels are written by the developers themselves.

Linking your bank to a budgeting app is convenient: transactions arrive on their own and categories fill in automatically. It also means a third-party data provider can read your accounts, your transaction history sits on another company's servers, and the app stops working well when the connection breaks. For some people, that trade isn't worth it.

This guide is for them. It covers why you might skip bank linking, what to check in an app before you trust it with your numbers, and the Android budget apps that work entirely on manual entry.

Why skip bank linking?

  • Privacy. Linked apps typically use a data aggregator. YNAB, for example, names MX and Plaid as its providers. Your transactions pass through that provider and are stored by the app.
  • Credentials. Many banks now support OAuth, where you sign in with the bank itself, but not all. YNAB's help pages note that with some banks you may need to enter your login credentials to link.
  • Fewer moving parts. No broken connections, duplicate transactions or missing imports to sort out.
  • Awareness. This is the argument behind kakeibo, the Japanese ledger method. Typing each purchase yourself makes you notice it in a way an automatic feed doesn't.

The cost is effort. You'll enter every expense, ideally the same day. If you know you won't, a linked app that you actually look at may serve you better than a manual one you abandon.

How to check an app's privacy before you install it

  1. Read the Data safety section on Google Play. It says whether the developer declares collecting or sharing data, and which types. Remember it's self-declared by the developer.
  2. Look for “Contains ads”. Ad-supported apps usually involve advertising SDKs, and their Data safety labels often list device or other IDs.
  3. Check whether an account is required. If you must sign up, your data is almost certainly stored on the developer's servers for sync.
  4. Check the permissions. On the app's Play page, open “About this app” and scroll to App permissions. An app without the “full network access” (internet) permission can't send anything anywhere by itself.
  5. Look at backups. A local-only app is only as safe as your backups. Prefer apps that let you export an encrypted file you control.

Kakeibo Ledger

Our app, so read this with that in mind. It follows the kakeibo method: plan the month, write each expense in one of four boxes (needs, wants, culture, unexpected), reflect at month end. It doesn't request Android's internet permission, so it can't go online; purchases go through the Google Play app on your phone. No account, no ads, no analytics. Backups are free, encrypted with a passphrase you choose, and saved wherever you pick through Android's share sheet. Free covers the whole method with the last three months of history; Pro (US$7.99, one time) adds unlimited history, a year in review, app lock, CSV export and themes.

Not for you if: you want custom categories, multiple currencies, account balances, or sync between devices or with a partner.

Cashew

A free, open-source (GPL-3.0) budget and expense tracker for Android, iOS and the web. Its listing says data stays on your device, with optional automatic Google Drive backups, and its Data safety label declares no data collected or shared. It's far more flexible than a kakeibo app: custom categories, budgets by week, month or any period, charts, multiple currencies with exchange rates, and bill reminders. It has a 4.8-star rating from about 28,000 reviews.

Not for you if: you want a guided method rather than a toolkit. The flexibility that makes Cashew great can also lead to the thirty-category setup that kakeibo tries to avoid.

Goodbudget

A long-running envelope budgeting app. The free plan (10 regular and 10 “more” envelopes, one account, two devices, one year of history) is manual entry only; automatic bank sync is a Premium feature that Goodbudget lists for US banks only. Its big strength is sharing: a household can use one budget across Android, iPhone and the web. That requires an account, and your budget is backed up to Goodbudget's servers.

Not for you if: you want your data to stay on your phone.

Money Manager (Realbyte)

One of the most popular manual-entry ledgers on Android, with over 10 million downloads. It uses double-entry bookkeeping, tracks accounts and cards, and has detailed statistics and Excel backups. The free version shows ads and limits you to 15 assets; a paid version removes both. Its Data safety label says it may collect and share some data types.

Not for you if: ads or data collection are the reason you're avoiding bank-linked apps.

YNAB, unlinked

YNAB lets you keep accounts unlinked and enter transactions by hand or import bank files. It's the most thorough option here for debts, credit cards and shared budgets. It's also a subscription and requires an account, with your budget in YNAB's cloud. See kakeibo vs YNAB for a fuller comparison.

Also worth a look

Actual Budget is open-source, local-first budgeting software you can run yourself; it works offline and has optional bank sync. It's aimed at people comfortable with a little self-hosting rather than a one-tap Play install. And a plain spreadsheet is still the most private option of all; our kakeibo template includes a spreadsheet layout.

Which one should you choose?

  • You want the most privacy and a simple method: Kakeibo Ledger, or a paper ledger.
  • You want a free, flexible tracker with charts: Cashew.
  • You share money with a partner: Goodbudget, or YNAB if you also manage debts.
  • You want accounts and bookkeeping detail and don't mind ads: Money Manager.

Making manual entry stick

Whichever app you choose, manual budgeting succeeds or fails on habit. Enter purchases the same day, check your bank statement once a week for anything you missed, and do a short review at month end. Our guide to tracking expenses by hand covers the routine in detail.

Questions

Is there a budget app that doesn't connect to your bank?

Yes. On Android, Kakeibo Ledger, Cashew, Money Manager and Goodbudget's free plan all work with manual entry, and YNAB can be used with unlinked accounts. You enter each expense yourself.

Is it safe to link my bank to a budgeting app?

Many people do so safely, especially with banks that support OAuth sign-in through the bank itself. It does mean a data provider and the app store your transactions. If you'd rather avoid that, use a manual-entry app.

How can I tell if an Android app can go online?

On the app's Google Play page, open About this app and scroll to App permissions. If full network access (the internet permission) isn't listed, the app can't open a network connection itself.

What is the most private budgeting app for Android?

The most private options keep data on the phone, need no account and declare no data collected. Kakeibo Ledger goes furthest by having no internet permission at all; Cashew also declares no data collected and is open source.

Are Google Play Data safety labels reliable?

They are a useful starting point, but they are written by each developer. Permissions, the privacy policy and whether an account is required give you a fuller picture.
