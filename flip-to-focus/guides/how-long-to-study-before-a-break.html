How long should you study before taking a break?

Updated 15 November 2026 · 7 min read
Short answerFor most students, somewhere between 25 and 50 minutes of focused study, then a 5 to 10-minute break, with a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes every two hours or so. There's no study that proves one perfect number. The evidence says regular short breaks leave you feeling less tired, longer breaks help more after demanding work, and the right block length depends on the task and on how easily you get started. Pick a length you can actually protect from interruptions, and adjust it from there.

Search for the ideal study interval and you'll find confident answers: 25 minutes, 52 minutes, 90 minutes. Each has a story behind it, but none comes from a study that compared study lengths for students and found a winner. Here's what the research does say, how to choose a block length for the work in front of you, and the signs that you need a break regardless of what the timer says.

All four can work. The useful question isn't which one is "right" but which one you can stick to for the work in front of you today.

What the research on breaks actually shows

Short breaks reduce tiredness

The best summary we have is a 2022 meta-analysis in PLOS ONE by Albulescu and colleagues, which pooled 22 samples with 2,335 participants. It looked at micro-breaks, defined as breaks of 10 minutes or less. Micro-breaks gave a small but reliable boost to how energetic people felt and reduced fatigue. Their effect on performance overall was small and not statistically significant; it was clearer for routine tasks than for cognitively demanding ones. Longer breaks were linked with bigger performance gains, and the authors suggest that recovering from very demanding work may need more than 10 minutes.

What that means for studying: short breaks are worth taking because they keep you fresher across a long day, but after hard problem-solving or a long stretch of revision, a 5-minute pause might not be enough.

Attention drifts on long, unbroken tasks

A frequently cited 2011 study by Ariga and Lleras in Cognition found that people doing a 50-minute attention task got steadily worse, but those who briefly switched to a different task a couple of times did not. The authors argued the brain gets used to a goal it holds for too long, and that brief diversions refresh it. A larger follow-up by Helton and Russell in 2012 didn't confirm the effect, so treat it as an interesting idea rather than settled fact.

Spacing beats cramming

Separate from breaks within a session, there's strong evidence about breaks between sessions. A 2013 review by Dunlosky and colleagues rated spacing study over days and weeks as one of the two most effective learning techniques they examined, alongside practice testing. A large 2008 experiment by Cepeda and colleagues found the best gap between reviews depends on how long you need to remember: the further away the exam, the longer the gap that works best. Three one-hour sessions on different days usually beat one three-hour session for the same material.

How to choose your study block

Essay writing, problem sets, coding50 min + 10 minIt takes time to load the problem into your head; short blocks cut you off as you get going.
A big project with a clear next step, and you're well rested90 min + 15 to 20 minSome people can sustain long blocks on absorbing work. Not ideal for a whole day.
Long exam revision dayBlocks of 25 to 50 min, with a 15 to 30-minute break every 2 hoursProtects you from the afternoon slump that comes from skipping rest.

A good test: if you regularly hit the end of a block and think "I've only just started", lengthen it. If you regularly find yourself drifting before the timer ends, shorten it. Our guide to the Pomodoro Technique explains the 25-minute version in detail.

Signs you need a break now

  • You've read the same paragraph twice without taking it in.
  • You've picked up your phone, or thought about it, more than once in the last few minutes.
  • You're making careless mistakes on things you know.
  • Your attention keeps sliding to what you'll do after studying.
  • You feel physically restless, hungry or thirsty.

If these show up early in a block, the problem may not be block length. It may be the task (too vague, too big) or the setting (phone in reach, noise). Fix those before you shorten the timer.

What makes a good study break

The Albulescu meta-analysis couldn't say which break activities work best, but the common-sense rule holds: rest the kind of attention you were using.

  • Good: standing, stretching, walking, water or a snack, looking out of a window, a few minutes outside.
  • Risky: social feeds, video and games. Not because they're forbidden, but because they're built to keep you going, and a 5-minute break that becomes 30 minutes wrecks the plan.
  • Useful on long breaks: food, a proper walk, a short chat with someone. Twenty minutes away from the desk is not wasted time.

Set a timer for the break as well as the study block. Ending a break on time is often harder than ending a study block.

How many hours a day can you study?

There's no reliable single number, and claims you'll see online (often borrowed from studies of musicians or chess players) don't transfer neatly to exam revision. In practice, many students find that around four to six hours of genuinely focused study, spread through the day with proper breaks, is the most they can do well, and on school or university days, much less. Count focused time honestly: two hours with your phone face down and out of reach usually beats five hours with it lighting up on the desk.

Track it for a week. You'll quickly see how many focused blocks you manage on a typical day, which makes planning a study schedule far more realistic.

Questions

Is it better to study for 25 or 50 minutes?

Neither is better in general. 25 minutes suits tasks that are hard to start or repetitive, like flashcards. 50 minutes suits work that takes time to get into, like essays and problem sets. Use the shorter block if you keep drifting, the longer one if you keep getting cut off.

How long should a study break be?

About 5 to 10 minutes after each study block, and a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes every two hours or so. Research suggests longer breaks help more after very demanding work.

Is the 52/17 rule backed by science?

Not by experiments. It comes from a productivity company's analysis of how its most productive users worked on their computers. It's a reasonable rhythm, but it wasn't tested against other intervals, and it wasn't about studying.

Should I take breaks if I feel focused?

Short ones, yes, especially on long study days: breaks reduce fatigue over the day. If you're in the middle of something, finish the thought or the problem, then stop. Don't skip the longer break every couple of hours.

Is it better to study in one long session or several short ones?

For remembering material, several shorter sessions spread over different days usually work better than one long session. This spacing effect is one of the most reliable findings in learning research.
