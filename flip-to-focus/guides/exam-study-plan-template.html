Exam study plan template: a countdown from eight weeks to exam day

Updated 15 November 2026 · 6 min read
Short answerStart from the exam date and work backwards. List every topic, rate how well you know each one, and estimate how many study blocks it needs. Split the time into three phases: learn and fill gaps, practise with past papers, then review and rest. Revisit each topic several times on different days rather than once, and test yourself instead of rereading. Below is a countdown template you can copy, with a worked example.

Most exam plans fail for one of two reasons: they're too vague ("revise chemistry") or too optimistic (eight hours a day, every day, from tomorrow). A good plan is specific, fits the time you really have, and is built around the two study techniques with the strongest evidence behind them: spacing your study over time and testing yourself. This guide walks you through building one, then gives you a copy-and-paste template.

Step 1: Count the time you really have

Write the exam date at the top of a page, then count the days left. Now be honest about each week: lessons, work, sport, family, the weekend you're away. For each day, write how many focused study blocks you can realistically do. A block is 25 to 50 minutes with a break after (see how long to study before a break).

A typical school or university week might give you two blocks on weekdays and four to six on weekend days, rising in the holidays. Add it up. That total is your budget, and it's almost always smaller than people expect, which is exactly why you need a plan.

Step 2: List every topic and rate it

Use the syllabus, course outline or exam board specification, not your memory of what was covered. For each topic, give a confidence rating:

  • Red: haven't learned it, or can't answer questions on it
  • Amber: understand it but make mistakes
  • Green: can answer exam questions on it reliably

Rate yourself by trying a few questions, not by how familiar your notes look. Familiarity is easy to confuse with knowing something.

Step 3: Estimate blocks per topic

A starting guide: red topics need four to six blocks, amber two to four, green one or two for review. Weight by how many marks a topic carries. If the total is more than your budget, cut green reviews to one block, and accept that some low-mark topics get less time. Deciding that now is much better than discovering it the night before.

Step 4: Split the countdown into three phases

PhaseWhen (8-week plan)FocusTypical activities
1. Learn and fill gapsWeeks 8 to 5 (D-56 to D-29)Red topics, then amberRelearn from notes or textbook, make flashcards, do worked examples, ask for help early
2. PractiseWeeks 4 to 2 (D-28 to D-8)Exam questions under time pressureTopic questions, then full past papers, marked honestly; log every mistake
3. Review and restFinal week (D-7 to D-1)Weak spots and confidenceMistake log, flashcards, one or two timed papers, sleep

With less time, compress the phases but keep all three. With four weeks: one week of gap-filling, two of practice, one of review.

Step 5: Space it out and test yourself

Two findings should shape every plan:

  • Spacing. A 2013 review by Dunlosky and colleagues rated distributed practice, spreading study of the same material across days and weeks, as one of the two most effective learning techniques. In a large 2008 experiment, Cepeda and colleagues found the best gap between reviews grows with how long you need to remember. So in your plan, each topic should appear several times, on different days, with growing gaps: for example, day 1, day 3, day 10, day 24.
  • Practice testing. The same review gave practice testing the other top rating. Roediger and Karpicke (2006) found that students who tested themselves remembered more a week later than those who restudied, even though restudying felt more effective at first. Make most of your blocks active: questions, flashcards from memory, explaining a topic out loud without notes.

Rereading and highlighting were rated low. They feel productive because the material looks familiar, but they don't build recall the way testing does.

A worked example

Maya has a biology final in eight weeks. She can do two blocks on weekdays and four on Saturdays, with Sundays mostly free except a weekly review. That's about 14 blocks a week, or 110 over eight weeks.

She lists 12 topics. Three are red (genetics, respiration, ecology), five amber and four green. Her estimate comes to 6 × 3 + 3 × 5 + 1.5 × 4 = 39 blocks for first passes, plus about 30 for spaced reviews and 25 for past papers: 94 blocks. That fits, with some slack for bad days.

In weeks 8 to 5, she does red topics first, each split across at least three days, with flashcards made in the first session and tested in later ones. Weeks 4 to 2 alternate topic questions with one full past paper each Saturday, marked against the mark scheme, with every lost mark logged. The final week is her mistake log, flashcards, one timed paper on D-4, and early nights.

The final week and exam day

  • Don't learn new topics in the last two or three days unless they carry big marks and you have nothing else to fix.
  • Do one timed paper in exam conditions, phone in another room.
  • Protect sleep. Late-night cramming trades tomorrow's recall for tonight's familiarity.
  • Prepare logistics the evening before: location, time, equipment, ID.
  • On the morning, a short look at your summary or flashcards is fine. Don't start anything long.

Questions

How far in advance should I start studying for an exam?

For a major exam, six to eight weeks gives you time to fill gaps, practise with past papers and review. For smaller tests, one to two weeks with spaced sessions is usually enough. Starting earlier lets you space reviews further apart, which improves long-term memory.

How many hours a day should I study for exams?

It depends on the time you have and your other commitments. Count focused blocks rather than hours: many students manage two to four blocks on school days and more on free days. Focused time with your phone away is worth far more than long hours with interruptions.

What is the most effective way to revise?

Testing yourself and spacing your study over time are the two techniques rated most effective in Dunlosky et al.'s 2013 review. Past papers, flashcards from memory and explaining topics without notes beat rereading and highlighting.

Should I study the night before an exam?

A light review is fine, but don't learn new material or stay up late. Sleep matters more than one more hour of cramming.
