The Pomodoro Technique: how to do it, and how long the breaks should be

Updated 15 November 2026 · 7 min read
Short answerA pomodoro is 25 minutes of work on one task, followed by a short break of about 3 to 5 minutes. After four pomodoros, take a longer break of about 15 to 30 minutes. Those are the timings Francesco Cirillo, who created the method, gives in his book. The timer is only half of it, though: the method also asks you to plan what you will work on, note every interruption, and record how many pomodoros each task took, so your estimates get better.

The Pomodoro Technique is probably the most widely copied study method there is, and also one of the most misremembered. Most people know "25 minutes on, 5 off". Fewer know why the breaks matter, what to do when you're interrupted, or that its creator describes it as a way of planning and estimating work, not just a timer. Here is the method as Cirillo describes it, how to run your first day, and how to adjust it for revision without losing what makes it work.

Where the Pomodoro Technique comes from

Francesco Cirillo started developing the method in the late 1980s as a university student. By his own account, he was struggling to concentrate while preparing for an exam, so he challenged himself to concentrate for just two minutes using a kitchen timer shaped like a tomato (pomodoro in Italian). The experiment grew into a method he later wrote up as a book, published in English by Currency in the US and Virgin Books in the UK.

His official site is clear that the technique is "more than 25 minutes": it connects focused work and breaks with planning, estimating effort, recording what happened and reviewing it. That matters for students, because the planning and recording are where most of the long-term benefit comes from.

The basic rules

  1. Choose one task. Something specific: "Biology chapter 4 flashcards", not "revise biology".
  2. Set a timer for 25 minutes and work only on that task until it rings.
  3. If something interrupts you, write it down and get back to the task. Don't act on it now unless it truly can't wait.
  4. When the timer rings, mark an X next to the task. That's one pomodoro.
  5. Take a short break of about 3 to 5 minutes.
  6. After four pomodoros, take a longer break of about 15 to 30 minutes.

Two rules people often miss. First, a pomodoro is indivisible: if you abandon it halfway to answer a message, it doesn't count, and you start a new one. Second, if you finish the task early, use the rest of the pomodoro to review what you just did rather than stopping the clock.

How long should Pomodoro breaks be?

Long breakAfter every four pomodoros (about two hours)About 15 to 30 minutesProper rest: a walk, food, fresh air. Let your mind wander.

The break is part of the method, not a reward you earn. Cirillo's site describes breaks as restorative and says they aren't "a reward for doing more". Skipping them because you feel on a roll tends to backfire after the third or fourth hour.

What counts as a break? Something that doesn't use the same kind of attention as the work. Scrolling a social feed is a poor choice: it's easy to start and hard to stop, and a 5-minute break that becomes 25 minutes breaks the rhythm. If you need your phone during a break, decide what you'll use it for before you pick it up.

Is there research on the exact numbers? Not on 25 and 5 specifically. A 2022 meta-analysis of "micro-breaks" (breaks of 10 minutes or less) found they made people feel more energetic and less tired, but the effect on performance was small and only significant for less demanding tasks; longer breaks tended to help more. Treat 25/5 as a sensible default, not a law. Our guide to how long to study before a break goes through the evidence.

Your first Pomodoro day, step by step

1. Make a list before you start

Cirillo uses two sheets: an Activity Inventory (everything you might work on) and a To Do Today sheet (what you choose for today, in order). For a student, the inventory is your syllabus, assignments and revision topics. The daily list should be short enough to finish.

2. Estimate in pomodoros

Next to each task, guess how many pomodoros it will take. If you guess more than five or six, break it into smaller tasks. If something will take less than one, group it with other small tasks.

3. Work, and track interruptions

Mark internal interruptions (the urge to check your phone, a sudden thought about dinner) with an apostrophe or a dash, and external ones (someone asks you something) with another symbol. You're not trying to be perfect. You're collecting evidence about what breaks your focus.

4. Record and review at the end of the day

Write down how many pomodoros each task really took, then compare with your guess. After a week, you'll know things like "a past paper takes me six pomodoros, not three", which is exactly the information you need to plan exam revision. Adapting the Pomodoro Technique for studying

Use the pomodoro for active study. Research on learning techniques rates practice testing and spacing study over time as the two most effective methods across ages and subjects, and rereading and highlighting as among the least. A pomodoro spent answering past-paper questions or doing flashcards from memory is worth more than one spent rereading notes.

Longer blocks can suit some work. If you're writing an essay or working through a long proof, 25 minutes may cut you off just as you get going. Many students use 50-minute sessions with 10-minute breaks for this kind of work. The principle is the same: a fixed, protected block, then a real break. Keep the 25-minute version for days when starting is the hard part.

Shorter can be fine too. If 25 minutes feels impossible, start with what you can do. Cirillo's own first experiment was a two-minute challenge. Build up as it gets easier.

Protect the pomodoro from your phone. The most common interruption for students is self-made: picking up the phone "just to check". Put it out of reach, turn on Do Not Disturb, or use a timer that only runs while the phone is face down. More ideas in how to stop checking your phone while studying.

Common mistakes

  • Pausing the timer. If you stop to reply to a message, the pomodoro is void. That rule is what makes the 25 minutes feel protected.
  • Skipping the long break. Four pomodoros is about two hours of focused work. Rest properly.
  • Vague tasks. "Study chemistry" invites drifting. "Do 10 equilibrium questions from the 2023 paper" doesn't.
  • Never reviewing. The recording is what turns the technique into better planning. Even a quick tally at the end of the day helps.
  • Treating it as a test of willpower. Cirillo's own advice is to be gentle with yourself and improve a little at a time.

Pen and paper, or an app?

Cirillo recommends starting with a mechanical timer, pencil and paper, and his site offers free worksheets. A physical timer has an advantage: winding it up is a small ritual that says "starting now", and it isn't a phone.

An app makes sense if your phone is the thing interrupting you, or if you want your sessions counted automatically. When choosing one, look for: no ads (an ad between sessions is an interruption), no account needed, a separate long-break setting, and a way to see your history. Questions

How long is a pomodoro?

25 minutes of work on a single task, followed by a short break. That is the length Francesco Cirillo uses in his book, though many students use longer blocks, such as 50 minutes, for writing or problem sets.

How long should a Pomodoro break be?

A short break of about 3 to 5 minutes after each pomodoro, and a longer break of about 15 to 30 minutes after every four pomodoros.

What should I do during a Pomodoro break?

Something that rests the kind of attention you were using: stand up, stretch, get water, look out of a window or walk around. Avoid starting anything that is hard to stop, like a social feed.

What happens if I'm interrupted during a pomodoro?

Note the interruption and get back to work if you can. If you have to stop and deal with it, the pomodoro doesn't count, and you start a fresh one afterwards.

Does the Pomodoro Technique work for ADHD?

Many people with ADHD find short, timed blocks with planned breaks helpful, and you can shorten the block if 25 minutes is too long at first. It's a productivity method, not a treatment, and there isn't strong research on it for ADHD specifically.

Is there a Pomodoro timer that stops me checking my phone?

Some timers only run while the phone is face down and pause when you pick it up, such as Flip to Focus on Android. Others block chosen apps during a session, such as Forest or Android's built-in Focus mode.
