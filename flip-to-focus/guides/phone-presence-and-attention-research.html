Does having your phone nearby hurt studying? What the research says

Updated 15 November 2026 · 6 min read
Short answerUsing your phone during study clearly hurts: notifications, even unanswered ones, disrupt attention, and switching back and forth leaves part of your mind on the interruption. Whether a silent phone just sitting on the desk drains your brain is much less certain. The famous 2017 "Brain Drain" study said yes, but a direct replication found no effect, and the two largest meta-analyses found either a small effect on working memory only or none at all. The safest setup is still the phone in another room; if it has to be near, make it silent and hard to use.

We make Flip to Focus, an Android study timer that runs only while your phone is face down. One of the studies below found face-down phones on a desk did no better than face-up ones, and we say so. Everything here comes from the papers linked at the end.

"Just having your phone on the desk makes you dumber" is one of the most shared findings in popular psychology. It comes from a real study, and it's a useful nudge. But the evidence has moved on since 2017, and the honest picture is more interesting: some phone effects are well supported, others are shaky. Knowing which is which helps you decide how strict you need to be.

The 2017 "Brain Drain" study

Adrian Ward, Kristen Duke, Ayelet Gneezy and Maarten Bos published "Brain Drain: The Mere Presence of One's Own Smartphone Reduces Available Cognitive Capacity" in the Journal of the Association for Consumer Research in 2017. Across two experiments with close to 800 smartphone users, participants did tests of working memory capacity and fluid intelligence while their phones were either on the desk, in a pocket or bag, or in another room. Phones were silenced, and participants didn't use them.

The headline result: people whose phones were in another room did better on the working-memory test than people whose phones were on the desk, with pocket or bag in between. The effect was strongest for people who said they depended most on their phones. In the University of Texas's write-up, the researchers noted it made no difference whether the phone was on or off, or face up or face down. Their explanation was that resisting the pull of a nearby phone uses up some of your limited attention, even when you succeed.

The study was widely covered and is still cited in school phone policies.

What happened when others tried to replicate it

Hartanto et al. (2024), Technology, Mind, and BehaviorFour-level meta-analysis, 33 studies, 4,368 participantsNo significant effect of mere presence overall (d = −0.02), and no difference by smartphone dependence or how presence was manipulated

So the specific claim that a silent, untouched phone on the desk saps your brainpower is, at best, a small effect on one kind of task, and possibly no effect. That doesn't mean phones are harmless while you study. It means the harm mostly comes from what phones do, not from their being there.

What is well supported

Notifications break attention, even unanswered

In a 2015 study in the Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance, Cary Stothart and colleagues had participants do a sustained-attention task while the researchers called or texted their phones. Participants didn't pick up. Those who got a notification were about three times as likely to make an error as those who didn't, a disruption the authors compared with that of actually using a phone.

Switching tasks leaves "attention residue"

Sophie Leroy's 2009 experiments found that when people move off an unfinished task, part of their attention stays behind, and they do worse on the next one. A quick look at a message mid-problem is a small task switch. Coming back, you're not quite where you were.

Interruptions make you faster but more stressed

Gloria Mark and colleagues found in 2008 that people who were interrupted finished tasks just as quickly, by working faster, but reported more stress, frustration and time pressure. For studying, where depth matters more than speed, that's a poor trade.

Schools that ban phones see modest gains

Louis-Philippe Beland and Richard Murphy studied English schools that banned phones and found exam scores rose by about 0.07 standard deviations afterwards, with the gains concentrated among lower-achieving students. That's a modest effect, and a ban removes use as well as presence, so it doesn't settle the mere-presence question. But it's consistent with phones being a distraction for students who struggle most to ignore them.

What it means for how you study

Putting the evidence together:

  1. Silence is non-negotiable. Notifications are the clearest harm. Turn on Do Not Disturb, or turn the phone off.
  2. Make checking hard. The real cost comes from picking the phone up and switching tasks. Distance, a bag, or a timer that penalises pick-ups all add friction.
  3. Another room is still the safe choice. Even if the mere-presence effect is small, a phone in another room can't be checked, can't buzz, and takes the decision away. If you're a heavy phone user, it costs little to be cautious.
  4. Don't panic if it has to be nearby. If you need the phone close, for safety, a login code or as your timer, the research suggests a silent, untouched phone is unlikely to wreck your focus. The habit of picking it up is the thing to work on.

Questions

Does having your phone on the desk make you less focused?

Possibly a little, but the evidence is mixed. The 2017 Brain Drain study found a phone on the desk lowered working-memory scores, but a 2022 direct replication found no effect, and 2024 meta-analyses found either a small effect on working memory only or none.

Does putting your phone face down help?

Against notifications and casual checking, yes. Against the mere-presence effect, Ward et al. (2017) found face down made no difference compared with face up. Another room is the safest place.

Is the Brain Drain effect real?

The original study was real and well run, but later work hasn't consistently reproduced it. A pre-registered replication in 2022 found no effect, Parry's 2024 meta-analysis found only a small working-memory effect, and Hartanto et al.'s 2024 meta-analysis found no significant effect overall.

Do notifications affect attention if I don't check them?

Yes. Stothart and colleagues (2015) found that receiving a call or text notification, without answering it, made errors on an attention task about three times as likely.

Do school phone bans improve grades?

Beland and Murphy found exam scores in English schools rose by about 0.07 standard deviations after phone bans, mostly among lower-achieving students. Other studies of bans have found mixed results.
