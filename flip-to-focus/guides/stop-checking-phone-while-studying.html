How to stop checking your phone while studying

Updated 15 November 2026 · 6 min read
Short answerMake checking your phone harder and less rewarding. The most effective single step is distance: put the phone in another room, or at least in a bag out of sight. Turn on Do Not Disturb so it can't call you back, decide in advance when you'll check it (in breaks, not mid-task), and use a timer or app that makes every pick-up visible. Expect to slip; the goal is fewer checks, not none.

We make Flip to Focus, an Android study timer that only runs while your phone is face down, which we mention as one option among several. Research findings here come from the published papers linked at the end.

Most phone checks during study aren't decisions. You hit a hard question, feel a flicker of boredom, and your hand is already on the phone. Telling yourself to "just use willpower" ignores how automatic that is. What works better is changing your setup so the automatic version is harder, and making the times you do check deliberate. Here are ten ways to do that, roughly from most to least effective.

Why you keep checking

Three things are usually going on:

  • Notifications pull you out. A 2015 study by Stothart and colleagues found that simply receiving a call or text notification, without touching the phone, made people about three times as likely to make errors on an attention task as people who got no notification.
  • Hard moments push you out. The urge to check spikes when the work gets difficult or dull. The phone offers a quick, easy reward.
  • Interruptions linger. A 2009 study by Sophie Leroy found that when people switch away from an unfinished task, part of their attention stays on it, and they do worse on the next thing. A "quick check" can cost more focus than the seconds it takes.

The fixes below target each of these.

1. Put the phone in another room

This is the strongest option if you can do it. In the well-known 2017 "Brain Drain" experiments by Ward and colleagues, students whose phones were in another room did better on working-memory tests than those whose phones were on the desk or in a pocket; whether the phone was face up or face down made no difference. Later replications and meta-analyses have found the "mere presence" effect is much smaller than first reported, or absent, but distance still removes the easy reach. Our guide to what the research says about phone presence covers this in detail.

2. If it must stay nearby, put it out of sight

If you need the phone for two-factor login, a family emergency or as your timer, put it in a bag, a drawer, or behind you. Out of sight means one fewer cue to check, and an extra step before you can.

3. Turn on Do Not Disturb, and allow only what matters

On Android, Do Not Disturb can still let through calls from starred contacts or repeat callers. Set that up once and you won't worry about missing an emergency, which removes one excuse to check. Turn it on at the start of every session, or let an app do it for you.

4. Turn off non-essential notifications for good

Go through your notification settings once and switch off everything that isn't from a person who needs you: shopping, games, news, social "someone liked your post". Fewer pings mean fewer pulls, during study and outside it.

5. Decide when you will check

"I won't look at my phone" is hard to keep. "I'll check messages in the 10-minute break at 4:00" is easier, because you're not denying the urge, just postponing it. Timed study blocks with planned breaks, like the Pomodoro Technique, build this in.

6. Write the urge down instead of acting on it

Keep a scrap of paper next to you. When you want to check something ("what's the weather tomorrow", "did she reply"), write it down and carry on. Look it up in the break. You'll often find you no longer care. This is the same trick Francesco Cirillo recommends for handling interruptions in the Pomodoro Technique.

7. Make each check visible

A lot of checking happens without noticing. Anything that makes a check visible helps: a tally mark on paper, Android's Digital Wellbeing unlock count at the end of the day, or a timer that pauses when you pick up the phone. Seeing "you checked 14 times in two hours" is a strong motivator.

8. Use friction: blockers and focus modes

Android has a built-in Focus mode (in Digital Wellbeing settings) that pauses the apps you choose, and you can schedule it. Apps like Forest also block chosen apps during a session. Blockers work well for people who drift into specific apps. Their weakness is that the phone is still in your hand, so checking time or messages is still easy, and any blocker can be switched off by the person who switched it on.

9. Use the phone face down, as the timer

Flip-style timers turn the phone itself into the commitment: the timer only runs while the phone lies face down, so picking it up visibly costs you. This helps if you use your phone as a study timer anyway, or need it nearby. It's less effective than putting the phone in another room, because the phone is still on the desk.

10. Study with other people

A library, a study group or a video call where everyone works quietly adds gentle social pressure, and it's awkward to scroll while the person across the table is working. Agree on break times together.

What to do when you slip

You will check your phone sometimes. Don't treat it as the end of the session. Put the phone back, note it, and finish the block. Look at the pattern at the end of the week: if most checks happen at the same point (half an hour in, or when you start past papers), that tells you where to put a break or which task needs breaking down.

And remember the phone isn't always the cause. If you keep reaching for it, the task may be too vague, too hard without help, or you may simply be tired. Our guide to how long to study before a break can help with the last one.

Questions

Is it better to put my phone face down or in another room?

Another room. In Ward et al.'s 2017 experiments, face up or face down on the desk made no difference, while a phone in another room was linked with better scores. Face down still helps against notifications and casual checking if the phone has to stay nearby.

Does Do Not Disturb help with studying?

Yes. Research by Stothart and colleagues found that even an unanswered notification hurts attention. Do Not Disturb removes those pings, and on Android you can still let through calls from starred contacts.

Do app blockers work?

They help people who lose time in specific apps, by adding friction. They don't stop you picking the phone up for other reasons, and you can switch them off, so they work best combined with other steps.

How many times do people check their phones?

Estimates vary a lot by study and method. A better number is your own: Android's Digital Wellbeing shows how many times you unlocked your phone each day.

Can a study timer app help me stop checking my phone?

It can, if it makes checking visible or costly. Flip-style timers, such as Flip to Focus, only run while the phone is face down and pause when you pick it up. Blocker-style timers, such as Forest, lock chosen apps during a session.
