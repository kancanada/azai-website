The Major Arcana: meanings of all 22 cards, for reflection

Updated 15 November 2026 · 13 min read
Short answerThe Major Arcana are the 22 trump cards of the tarot, numbered 0 (the Fool) to XXI (the World). They stand for big themes rather than daily details: beginnings, choices, endings, hope, completion. Below, each card has a description of its Rider-Waite-Smith image, what it's commonly taken to mean as a prompt for reflection, and one question to ask yourself when you draw it.

We make Daily Oracle, a free Android app (with ads) that draws one card a day from the 1909 Rider-Waite-Smith deck. The meanings below were written for this guide; they're one reader's take, not the only one.

The 78 cards of a tarot deck divide into two groups: 56 Minor Arcana in four suits, which tend to describe everyday situations, and 22 Major Arcana, which describe larger themes. When a Major Arcana card turns up in a daily pull (by chance, a little over one day in four), it's often worth slowing down for.

These descriptions follow the Rider-Waite-Smith deck, drawn by Pamela Colman Smith in 1909, because its images are the ones most readers know. We describe what's in each picture first, because looking closely at the image is the best way to learn a card. Then we give what the card is commonly taken to mean, and a question to take into your day. We treat the cards as prompts for reflection, not predictions.

How to use this list

  • Look at the card before reading. If you have a deck, find the card and spend a minute with it. What do you notice that we haven't mentioned?
  • Take the question, not the verdict. The question at the end of each card is the useful part. Write one line in answer to it.
  • Hold meanings loosely. Tarot meanings vary between readers and books. If a card means something different to you, that's a real reading, not a wrong one.

Many teachers describe the Major Arcana as the “Fool's journey”: the Fool, card 0, meets each of the other cards in turn, from the Magician's skill to the World's completion. It's a modern teaching device rather than something Waite wrote, but it's a helpful way to remember the order.

The 22 Major Arcana

0 · The Fool

beginnings · trust · a leap

A young traveller steps toward a cliff edge with a white rose in one hand, a small bundle on a staff, and a little dog at his heels, his eyes on the sky rather than the drop. The Fool is the card of starting before you feel ready. It can be read as healthy openness, the willingness to try something without knowing how it ends, or as carelessness, depending on what you bring to it. Ask whether you're holding back out of wisdom or out of fear of looking foolish.

Reflect: Where would a beginner's mind help me today?

I · The Magician

skill · focus · using what you have

A figure raises a wand to the sky and points the other hand to the ground. On the table in front of him lie a cup, a sword, a pentacle and a wand, the symbols of all four suits. Everything needed is already on the table. The Magician is about turning intention into action with the tools you actually have, rather than the ones you wish you had. It also asks about honesty: skill can be used to make things or to perform tricks.

Reflect: What's one thing I can make happen today with what's already in front of me?

II · The High Priestess

intuition · stillness · what isn't said

A calm woman sits between a black pillar and a white one, a partly hidden scroll in her lap, a crescent moon at her feet and a veil behind her. She doesn't act; she knows. This is the card of quiet attention: listening before speaking, noticing what you feel before deciding what you think. It can be a nudge to give a situation more time, or to trust a hunch you've been arguing yourself out of.

Reflect: What do I already know about this that I haven't said out loud?

III · The Empress

nurture · abundance · the senses

A woman sits on cushions in a ripe field of wheat, with trees and a stream behind her and a heart-shaped shield bearing the symbol of Venus at her side. The Empress is about growth that comes from care rather than force: gardens, meals, projects and relationships that flourish when they're tended. She also points to the body and the senses. In a busy week she can be a reminder that rest and pleasure are part of making things, not a reward for finishing them.

Reflect: What in my life would grow if I gave it a little more care?

IV · The Emperor

structure · authority · responsibility

A stern ruler sits on a stone throne carved with rams' heads, armour showing under his robe, bare mountains behind him. Where the Empress grows things, the Emperor organises them. This card is about rules, plans, boundaries and the person responsible for them, which might be you. Read generously, it's the stability that lets other things thrive. Read critically, it's rigidity and control. It's worth asking which one you're seeing.

Reflect: Where would a clear rule or plan make today easier?

V · The Hierophant

tradition · teaching · belonging

A religious figure raises a hand in blessing over two kneeling followers, with crossed keys at his feet. The Hierophant stands for institutions, traditions and the teachers who pass them on: schools, faiths, professions, families. It invites you to think about what you've inherited and what you've chosen. Sometimes the wise move is to learn the established way first; sometimes it's to notice a rule you follow only because you were told to.

Reflect: Which tradition or teacher am I drawing on, and is it still serving me?

VI · The Lovers

choice · values · relationship

Two figures stand beneath a winged figure in a bright sky, a tree of flames behind one and a fruit tree with a serpent behind the other. Despite the name, this card is as much about choosing as about romance. It asks what you value, and whether your choices line up with it. In a relationship it can point to honesty and partnership; on its own, to a decision you can't make halfway.

Reflect: What choice in front of me is really a choice about what I value?

VII · The Chariot

willpower · direction · momentum

An armoured figure stands in a chariot under a starry canopy, drawn by two sphinxes, one black and one white, with a city behind. There are no visible reins: control comes from will and focus, not from pulling. The Chariot is about moving forward when parts of you want different things. It's a card of discipline and direction, and also a warning that going fast isn't the same as knowing where you're going.

Reflect: Where am I going today, and what's pulling me off course?

VIII · Strength

gentle courage · patience · self-control

A woman calmly closes a lion's jaws with her hands, a garland of flowers around her and an infinity sign above her head. There's no struggle in the picture. Strength in this deck means the quiet kind: patience with yourself, kindness under pressure, steadiness with strong feelings rather than suppression of them. In the Rider-Waite-Smith deck this card is VIII; in older decks it's XI (see our guide to the deck's history).

Reflect: Where could patience do more than force today?

IX · The Hermit

solitude · reflection · inner guidance

An old man in a grey cloak stands on a snowy peak, holding up a lantern with a star inside it and leaning on a staff. He has stepped away from the crowd, but he's holding the light up as if for someone climbing behind him. The Hermit is about chosen solitude: time to think, study or simply be quiet. It isn't loneliness. It can be a prompt to protect some time alone, or to share what you've learned once you've learned it.

Reflect: What would I understand better with an hour of quiet?

X · Wheel of Fortune

cycles · change · what's outside your control

A great wheel turns in the sky, marked with letters and symbols, with a sphinx on top, a snake on one side and a jackal-headed figure on the other, and four winged creatures reading in the corners. Things rise and things fall. The Wheel is the card of seasons and turning points, and of the large part of life you don't control. It pairs well with the Stoic idea of separating what's up to you from what isn't.

Reflect: What's changing that I can't stop, and what's my part in it?

XI · Justice

fairness · truth · consequences

A figure sits between two pillars holding an upright sword in one hand and balanced scales in the other. Justice is about cause and effect, honest accounting and fair decisions. It can point to a literal agreement or decision, but more often in a daily pull it asks whether you're being fair: to others, and to yourself. It's also the card of owning the results of your choices. In older decks Justice is VIII.

Reflect: Where do I need to be more honest about cause and effect?

XII · The Hanged Man

pause · surrender · a new angle

A man hangs upside down by one foot from a living tree, his hands behind his back, a halo around his head and a calm, almost peaceful expression. He isn't suffering; he's waiting, and seeing the world from a different angle. The Hanged Man is about deliberate pauses: letting go of a struggle, waiting for something to ripen, or giving up a small thing to understand a bigger one.

Reflect: What would I see if I looked at this problem upside down?

XIII · Death

endings · transition · clearing space

A skeleton in black armour rides a white horse, carrying a black banner with a white rose; a king lies fallen and others kneel, while the sun rises between two towers in the distance. This is the most misunderstood card in the deck. In reflective tarot it almost never means a literal death; it stands for endings and the change they make possible. Something has finished, or needs to, so something else can begin.

Reflect: What is already ending, and what might its ending make room for?

XIV · Temperance

balance · moderation · blending

A winged figure stands with one foot on land and one in a pool, pouring water between two cups, with irises at the edge and a path leading to a bright crown of light in the hills. Temperance is the card of patient mixing: combining different things in the right measure until they work together. It suggests moderation, but not dullness. Think of a cook adjusting a recipe, or a person finding the middle way between two extremes.

Reflect: What in my day needs a little less, and what needs a little more?

XV · The Devil

habits · attachment · what holds you

A horned figure squats on a pedestal above two chained figures who have grown horns and tails of their own. Look closely and the chains around their necks are loose: they could lift them off. The Devil is about the habits, cravings and stories that keep us stuck, and the uncomfortable fact that we often consent to them. It isn't about evil. It's an honest look at what has more hold on you than you'd like.

Reflect: What am I chained to that I could actually take off?

XVI · The Tower

disruption · revelation · what can't stand

Lightning strikes a tall tower, knocking off its crown; flames pour from the windows and two figures fall. The Tower looks frightening, but in reflection it's about a structure that was never sound: a false belief, a shaky plan, a pretence. When it falls, it's painful and also clarifying. In a daily pull it can prompt you to look at what you've been propping up that you know won't hold.

Reflect: What am I propping up that isn't really standing?

XVII · The Star

hope · renewal · calm after a storm

After the Tower comes the Star: a woman kneels at a pool under a bright star and seven smaller ones, pouring water onto the land and back into the pool, with a bird in a tree behind her. She's unguarded and unhurried. This card is about recovery and quiet hope, the steady kind that doesn't need proof. It's a good card for remembering what you're working toward and giving yourself a little of what you give others.

Reflect: What small thing would renew me today?

XVIII · The Moon

uncertainty · imagination · unclear paths

A moon with a face shines between two towers; a dog and a wolf howl at it, a crayfish climbs out of a pool, and a path winds off into the distance. Nothing is clear by moonlight. This card is about uncertainty, dreams, fears and the imagination, which can both mislead and inspire. It asks you to notice where you're filling in gaps with worry, and to keep walking the path even when you can't see its end.

Reflect: Where am I filling in the unknown with fear rather than facts?

XIX · The Sun

joy · clarity · openness

A child rides a white horse beneath a huge sun, arms wide, with sunflowers growing over a wall behind. This is one of the simplest and brightest cards in the deck: warmth, energy, being seen and seeing clearly. In a daily pull it often invites you to enjoy something without overthinking it, or to be more open and direct about something you've kept in the shade.

Reflect: What can I enjoy fully today, without needing it to be more?

XX · Judgement

awakening · reckoning · a calling

An angel sounds a trumpet from the clouds and figures rise from their coffins with arms raised. Judgement is about waking up: hearing a call, taking stock of your life honestly, and answering. It's less about being judged by others than about your own clear-eyed review, followed by a decision to change. It can point to forgiveness, of yourself and others, as part of moving on.

Reflect: What is asking for my attention that I've been sleeping through?

XXI · The World

completion · integration · wholeness

A dancing figure holds two wands inside a large oval wreath, with an angel, an eagle, a bull and a lion in the four corners. The World is the end of the Major Arcana's journey: a cycle complete, the parts working together. It's a card for marking finished things, celebrating them and noticing what you've learned before the next beginning, which in the deck's logic is the Fool again.

Reflect: What have I finished, and how can I mark it?

Patterns worth noticing

Learning 22 separate meanings is easier if you see how they relate. A few pairings and sequences come up again and again:

  • The Empress and the Emperor (III and IV): growing things and organising them. Most lives need both.
  • The High Priestess and the Hierophant (II and V): private, inner knowing and shared, taught knowledge. Both sit between two pillars.
  • The Tower, the Star, the Moon and the Sun (XVI to XIX): disruption, then hope, then uncertainty, then clarity. Read in order, they're a fair description of how recovering from a setback often feels.
  • Death and Judgement (XIII and XX): an ending, and later a reckoning and a new start. Neither is literal.
  • The Fool and the World (0 and XXI): the beginning and the end of the cycle, which loops back to begin again.

A note on numbering

If your deck numbers Strength as XI and Justice as VIII, it follows the older Tarot de Marseille order. A. E. Waite swapped the two cards for the 1909 deck to fit the astrological correspondences of the Golden Dawn. Neither order is wrong. The history of the Rider-Waite-Smith deck explains why.

Questions

How many Major Arcana cards are there?

22: the Fool, numbered 0, and 21 numbered cards from I (the Magician) to XXI (the World). The rest of a 78-card tarot deck is the 56 Minor Arcana.

What is the difference between the Major and Minor Arcana?

The Major Arcana are 22 trump cards with their own names that stand for big themes. The Minor Arcana are 56 cards in four suits (Wands, Cups, Swords and Pentacles) that tend to describe everyday situations.

Does the Death card mean someone will die?

No. In reflective tarot, Death stands for endings and transitions: something finishing so something else can begin. Tarot isn't a way to predict events, including deaths.

Is Strength card 8 or card 11?

In the Rider-Waite-Smith deck and most decks based on it, Strength is VIII and Justice is XI. In the older Tarot de Marseille order, Justice is VIII and Strength is XI.

What is the Fool's journey?

A modern way of teaching the Major Arcana as a story: the Fool (0) meets each card in turn, from the Magician to the World, learning something from each. It's a memory aid, not part of Waite's original writing.
