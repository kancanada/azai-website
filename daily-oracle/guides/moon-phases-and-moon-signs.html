Moon phases explained, and what a moon sign is

Updated 15 November 2026 · 8 min read
Short answerThe Moon has no light of its own: the Sun always lights half of it, and as the Moon orbits Earth we see more or less of that lit half. That gives eight named phases, from new moon to full moon and back, in a cycle of about 29.5 days. A “moon sign” is an astrological idea, not an astronomical one: it's the zodiac sign the Moon is passing through, using twelve 30-degree slices of the sky. The Moon moves through a sign in about two and a half days.

We make Daily Oracle, a free Android app (with ads) that shows the moon phase and moon sign for your date; we mention it near the end. The astronomy here comes from NASA and the U.S. Naval Observatory; moon signs are astrology, which has no scientific basis.

The Moon is the easiest thing in the night sky to watch change. Over a month it grows from nothing to a full bright disc and shrinks back again, and people have organised calendars, festivals and farming around that rhythm for thousands of years. Here's what's actually happening, what the numbers in a moon app mean, and what people mean when they talk about the Moon being “in Leo”.

Why the Moon has phases

The Moon doesn't glow; it reflects sunlight. At any moment the Sun lights exactly half of it, the half facing the Sun. As the Moon travels around Earth, we see that lit half from a changing angle. When the Moon is roughly between Earth and the Sun, the lit side faces away from us and we see almost nothing: a new moon. When Earth is roughly between the Moon and the Sun, we see the whole lit side: a full moon. Everything in between is a phase.

A common misconception is that phases are caused by Earth's shadow. They aren't. Earth's shadow falls on the Moon only during a lunar eclipse, which happens at some full moons, not every month, because the Moon's orbit is tilted relative to Earth's.

The eight moon phases

Astronomers name four main phases (new, first quarter, full, third quarter) and four in-between ones. The descriptions of what you see below are for the Northern Hemisphere; south of the equator, the lit side is flipped left to right.

“Waxing” means growing and “waning” means shrinking. “Gibbous” comes from a Latin word for hump-backed, describing the bulging shape between half and full. Strictly, new, first quarter, full and third quarter are instants, precise moments in time; the other four are stretches of several days between them.

How long is a lunar cycle?

There are two answers, and both are right.

  • About 29.5 days from one new moon to the next. This is the synodic month, the cycle of phases. NASA puts it at about 29.5 days.
  • About 27.3 days for the Moon to go once around Earth relative to the distant stars. This is the sidereal month; NASA gives 27.322 days.

The difference exists because Earth is moving around the Sun at the same time. After one full orbit, the Moon has to travel a little further, about two extra days, to get back into the same position relative to the Sun and show the same phase again.

The 29.5-day cycle also explains why a calendar month usually has one full moon but occasionally two, and why the date of the full moon drifts earlier by about a day each month.

What “illumination” and “moon age” mean

Moon apps and almanacs often show two extra numbers:

  • Illumination is the percentage of the Moon's visible disc that's lit. It's 0% at new moon, 50% at the quarters and 100% at full moon. It changes fastest around the quarters and slowest near new and full, so the Moon looks “almost full” for a couple of days either side.
  • Moon age is the number of days since the last new moon. A moon about 7.4 days old is near first quarter, about 14.8 days old is near full, and about 22 days old is near third quarter.

Is the phase the same everywhere on Earth?

Yes. A full moon happens at a single instant for the whole planet. What differs is the local clock time and sometimes the date: a full moon at 2:00 a.m. in London falls on the previous evening in Vancouver. The view also differs by hemisphere. A waxing crescent looks like a backwards “C” in the north, lit on the right, but lit on the left in Australia or South Africa. Near the equator, crescents can look like a smile.

Why we always see the same face

The Moon spins on its axis once for every orbit around Earth, so the same side always faces us. NASA describes this as being tidally locked. The far side isn't permanently dark, though: it gets as much sunlight as the near side over a month, and at new moon it's fully lit. “Dark side of the Moon” really means the side we never see, not a side without light.

What is a moon sign?

Here we leave astronomy for astrology. A moon sign is the zodiac sign the Moon is passing through at a given moment. Western astrology divides the Sun's yearly path across the sky, the ecliptic, into twelve equal 30-degree slices named after the zodiac signs, starting with Aries at the position of the Sun at the March equinox. This is called the tropical zodiac. The Moon travels close to the same path, so at any moment it's in one of those twelve slices.

The Moon moves quickly: about 13 degrees a day on average, varying between roughly 12 and 15 as its distance from Earth changes. So it crosses a 30-degree sign in a little over two days, usually somewhere between two and two and a half, and goes through all twelve signs in about 27.3 days. That's why the moon sign changes every couple of days, while the Sun spends a month in each sign.

People use the idea in two ways. “My moon sign” usually means the sign the Moon was in when they were born. “The moon is in Taurus today” refers to where it is now, and some people use it as a theme for the day: practical and steady in Taurus, social in Gemini, and so on.

Signs and constellations aren't the same thing

Because the tropical zodiac is tied to the March equinox, and Earth's axis slowly wobbles over thousands of years (precession), the signs no longer line up with the constellations they're named after. When the Moon is “in Aries” by the tropical zodiac, it's usually in front of the stars of Pisces. Some traditions, including Hindu astrology, use a sidereal zodiac tied to the stars instead, so their signs differ by most of a sign from the Western ones.

What a moon sign is, and isn't

The Moon's position is real, measurable astronomy. The meaning attached to each sign is astrology, and scientific tests have found no evidence that astrological signs predict events or personality. NASA puts it plainly: astrology's claims are not based on scientific evidence. If you use the daily moon sign, use it the way you'd use a tarot card: as a theme to reflect on, not a forecast.

Using the moon as a reflection calendar

Whatever you think of astrology, the lunar cycle makes a handy rhythm for checking in with yourself. The new moon and full moon are about two weeks apart, so they're natural points to set an intention and to review it. Some ideas:

  • New moon: pick one small habit to try until the next new moon.
  • First quarter: a quick check: is the habit happening? What's getting in the way?
  • Full moon: review what's become clearer in the last two weeks.
  • Third quarter: decide what to drop before the next cycle.

Questions

What are the 8 phases of the moon?

New moon, waxing crescent, first quarter, waxing gibbous, full moon, waning gibbous, third (or last) quarter and waning crescent. The cycle takes about 29.5 days.

Why does the moon have phases?

The Sun always lights half of the Moon. As the Moon orbits Earth, we see different amounts of that lit half. Phases aren't caused by Earth's shadow; that only happens during a lunar eclipse.

How long does the moon stay in each zodiac sign?

A little over two days on average, usually between two and two and a half, because the Moon moves about 13 degrees a day and each sign spans 30 degrees. It passes through all twelve signs in about 27.3 days.

What is my moon sign?

In astrology, your moon sign is the zodiac sign the Moon was in when you were born, worked out from your birth date, time and place. Astrology has no scientific basis; many people use it as a prompt for reflection.

Is the moon phase the same everywhere?

Yes, each main phase happens at the same instant worldwide, but the local time and sometimes the date differ. In the Southern Hemisphere the lit side appears flipped compared with the Northern Hemisphere.
