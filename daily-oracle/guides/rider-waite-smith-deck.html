The Rider-Waite-Smith tarot: its history, its artist, and its public-domain status

Updated 15 November 2026 · 7 min read
Short answerThe Rider-Waite-Smith deck was published in London in December 1909 by William Rider & Son. The occultist A. E. Waite planned it, and the artist Pamela Colman Smith drew all 78 cards, including scenes on every numbered card, which made it the template for most tarot decks since. The original 1909 artwork is in the public domain in the United States and in countries with a copyright term of the author's life plus 70 years or less, such as the UK; later recoloured editions and the “Rider-Waite” trademark are a separate matter.

We make Daily Oracle, a free Android app (with ads) that uses scans of the original 1909 Rider-Waite-Smith cards; we mention it near the end. Nothing here is legal advice.

If you picture a tarot card, you're probably picturing one drawn by Pamela Colman Smith: the Fool stepping toward a cliff edge with a small white dog, the figure on the Ten of Swords, the cups lined up in front of the mourner on the Five of Cups. Her deck, usually called the Rider-Waite or Rider-Waite-Smith tarot, is well over a century old and remains the reference point for most modern decks. Here's where it came from, what made it different, and what its copyright status actually is.

The two people behind it

Arthur Edward Waite

A. E. Waite was born in Brooklyn, New York, in 1857 and grew up in England. He became a prolific writer on mysticism, ritual magic and occult history, and in 1891 he joined the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, the London society whose members studied ceremonial magic, astrology, Kabbalah and tarot. He later led his own branch of it. Waite died in 1942.

Waite conceived the deck and directed its symbolism. He also wrote its companion book: The Key to the Tarot in 1909, expanded in 1911 as The Pictorial Key to the Tarot, with black-and-white plates of all 78 of Smith's designs. That book is still in print and freely available online.

Pamela Colman Smith

Pamela Colman Smith was born in Pimlico, London, in 1878, and spent parts of her childhood in Jamaica and the United States. Friends called her “Pixie”. She worked as an illustrator, a stage and costume designer with Ellen Terry, Henry Irving and Bram Stoker's Lyceum Theatre company, and a storyteller who performed Jamaican folk tales. W. B. Yeats introduced her to the Golden Dawn, which she joined in 1901.

In 1907 the photographer Alfred Stieglitz showed her paintings at his Little Galleries of the Photo-Secession in New York, the gallery later known as 291. It was the first time the gallery, until then devoted to photography, had shown a painter. Two years later Waite asked her to illustrate his tarot. Most accounts say she was paid a flat fee and received no royalties from a deck that went on to sell in enormous numbers. She died in Bude, Cornwall, in 1951.

For decades the deck was known by its publisher's and its author's names, “Rider-Waite”, and Smith was barely mentioned. Her small monogram, a P crossed with a looping C and S, appears on the cards. Many historians and publishers now call it the Waite-Smith or Rider-Waite-Smith tarot to give her credit.

Publication in 1909

William Rider & Son of London published the deck in December 1909. The first printing was small. A larger printing followed in March 1910, and Rider kept the deck in print in various editions until 1939, then again in the 1970s. The very first box was simply labelled “Tarot Cards”.

Smith completed all 78 designs in 1909, the same year Waite commissioned them. Early printings were coloured with flat, fairly muted tones; many modern editions are recoloured with brighter, more saturated colours, which is worth knowing if you're comparing decks or looking for the original art.

What made the deck new

Scenes on every card

Most earlier tarot decks, like the Tarot de Marseille, showed the numbered “pip” cards of the Minor Arcana the way playing cards do: the Seven of Cups was simply seven cups arranged in a pattern. Smith gave every one of the 56 Minor Arcana cards a scene with people doing something. The Three of Pentacles is a craftsman at work in a church. The Eight of Cups is a figure walking away from a stack of cups under the moon.

That one decision changed how tarot is read. A beginner can look at the Five of Swords, see one figure collecting swords while two others walk away dejected, and start interpreting without a book. It's the main reason the deck is still recommended to beginners, and why so many later decks keep her compositions while changing the art.

She had at least one precedent. The Sola Busca tarot, engraved in Italy in the late 15th century, is the earliest known deck in which all the suit cards are illustrated. In 1907 photographs of it were given to the British Museum, and Waite and Smith probably saw them; a few of Smith's Minor Arcana compositions closely echo Sola Busca cards.

Strength and Justice swap places

In older decks, Justice is card VIII and Strength is card XI. Waite swapped them, making Strength VIII and Justice XI, so that the cards fitted the astrological correspondences taught in the Golden Dawn: Strength with Leo and Justice with Libra. Most decks based on Rider-Waite-Smith follow his order, while decks in the Marseille tradition keep the older one. If you ever see two numbering schemes, that's why.

A deliberate symbolic programme

Waite was explicit that the images contained layers of symbolism, some of which he chose not to explain in print. Smith's designs draw on that, and on her own theatrical eye: many cards are composed like stage scenes, with a figure facing the audience and a backdrop behind. The colour choices (yellow skies, grey skies, red and white roses and lilies) recur across the deck and reward close looking.

Why it became the standard

Several things came together. The deck was in English, published with a readable guidebook, at a time when interest in tarot was growing in the English-speaking world. The illustrated Minor Arcana made it far easier to learn than pip-only decks. And from 1971 the American publisher U.S. Games Systems produced and distributed a recoloured edition on a very large scale, which put the deck in bookshops for generations of new readers. Today a large share of tarot books, courses and apps assume Rider-Waite-Smith imagery.

Is the Rider-Waite-Smith deck in the public domain?

This is the question artists, publishers and app makers ask most. Here's the situation in plain terms, with the caveat that copyright depends on the country and the specific edition, and this isn't legal advice.

The name “Rider-Waite”Treated by U.S. Games Systems as its trademark; trademark law is separate from copyright.

In practice, people who want to reuse the art work from scans of early printings, not from modern recoloured editions, and describe the deck in general terms rather than using a trademarked name as a product name. If you're planning a commercial product, ask a lawyer in your country.

How to look at the cards more closely

If you own a Rider-Waite-Smith deck, try this: pick one card a day and spend two minutes describing it before reading any meaning. Where's the light coming from? What's in the background? Which way is the figure facing? Smith put a remarkable amount into small details, and noticing them is the fastest way to learn the deck. Questions

Who illustrated the Rider-Waite tarot deck?

Pamela Colman Smith, an artist born in London in 1878, drew all 78 cards in 1909 to A. E. Waite's instructions. That's why the deck is increasingly called the Rider-Waite-Smith or Waite-Smith tarot.

When was the Rider-Waite-Smith deck first published?

In December 1909, by William Rider & Son of London, with a larger printing in March 1910. Waite's guidebook followed as The Key to the Tarot (1909) and The Pictorial Key to the Tarot (1911).

Is the Rider-Waite tarot public domain?

The original 1909 artwork is in the public domain in the US, where works published before 1931 are out of copyright, and in life-plus-70 countries such as the UK, since Smith died in 1951. Later recoloured editions and the “Rider-Waite” trademark are separate. This isn't legal advice.

Why are Strength and Justice numbered differently in some decks?

Waite made Strength card VIII and Justice card XI to fit Golden Dawn astrological correspondences (Strength with Leo, Justice with Libra). Older decks such as the Tarot de Marseille have Justice as VIII and Strength as XI.

What is the difference between Rider-Waite and Rider-Waite-Smith?

They're the same deck. “Rider-Waite-Smith” or “Waite-Smith” adds the name of the artist, Pamela Colman Smith, who was long left out of the deck's name.
