Tarot vs oracle cards: what's the difference?

Updated 15 November 2026 · 7 min read
Short answerTarot is a fixed system: 78 cards, with 22 Major Arcana and 56 Minor Arcana in four suits, so what you learn from one deck mostly carries over to the next. Oracle cards have no fixed system: each deck's creator decides how many cards there are, what they show and what they mean, and many print the message on the card. Choose tarot if you want a shared language to learn over years; choose an oracle deck if you want something you can use straight away with no study.

We make Daily Oracle, a free Android app (with ads) that we mention near the end. Despite its name, it uses a tarot deck, not an oracle deck; this guide explains the difference.

Walk into any bookshop's card section and you'll see two kinds of boxes: tarot decks and oracle decks. They look similar, they're both shuffled and drawn, and people use both for reflection. But they're built very differently, and that difference decides how you learn them, how you read them and how much one deck has in common with the next.

What makes a deck “tarot”

A tarot deck has a fixed skeleton. There are 22 Major Arcana, also called trumps: the Fool, unnumbered or numbered zero, and 21 numbered cards from the Magician to the World. Then there are 56 Minor Arcana, in four suits of 14 cards each. In most modern decks the suits are Wands, Cups, Swords and Pentacles (sometimes called Rods, Chalices, Blades or Coins). Each suit runs from Ace to Ten, followed by four court cards: Page, Knight, Queen and King.

That skeleton is old. The earliest documented tarot decks were recorded in northern Italy between about 1440 and 1450, and the oldest surviving cards are the hand-painted Visconti-Sforza decks made for the rulers of Milan. For their first three centuries, tarot cards were used to play trick-taking games, and those games are still played in parts of Europe. Historians find no real evidence of tarot being used for divination until the late 18th century, when French writers such as Antoine Court de Gébelin (in 1781) and Etteilla popularised the idea that the cards held ancient esoteric meaning.

The structure is what makes tarot learnable. Because every deck has a Three of Swords and a Hermit, a book written for one deck mostly works for another. Artists reinterpret the cards, sometimes radically, but the reader knows where each card sits in the system: its suit, its number and its place in the sequence of the Major Arcana.

What makes a deck an “oracle” deck

“Oracle deck” is a loose label for any deck of cards made for reflection or divination that isn't tarot. There's no fixed number of cards, no suits and no shared meanings. One deck might have 44 cards of animals, another 52 cards of goddesses, another 36 cards of plants with a single word printed on each. The creator decides the theme, the number and the meaning of each card, and explains it in the booklet.

That freedom is the appeal. An oracle deck can be built around anything: affirmations, seasons, a mythology, a particular artist's world. Because the message is often printed on the card, you can pick one up and use it without any study at all.

The trade-off is that nothing transfers. Learning one oracle deck tells you almost nothing about the next one. And because the meaning is usually fixed by the text, there's less room for the slow, personal interpretation that tarot readers value.

Where Lenormand fits

Lenormand cards sit between the two. A Lenormand deck has 36 cards with simple, everyday images: a ship, a house, a tree, clouds, a snake, a key. The deck is named after Marie Anne Lenormand, a famous French fortune-teller of the Napoleonic era who died in 1843, but she didn't design it. The imagery traces back to a German parlour game, The Game of Hope, designed by Johann Kaspar Hechtel and published in 1799. Publishers attached Lenormand's name after her death to sell the cards.

Lenormand is a system, like tarot: the 36 cards have broadly agreed meanings, and readers combine pairs of cards like words in a sentence. So it's often grouped with oracle cards in shops, but it reads quite differently from a modern oracle deck.

How reading them feels different

Tarot invites interpretation. Most tarot cards show a scene without explaining it. The Four of Cups shows a figure under a tree, arms crossed, ignoring a cup offered from a cloud. Is that boredom? Contemplation? Stubbornness? You decide, based on the picture, its place in the system and your situation. That's why tarot suits journaling: there's always something to think through.

Oracle cards deliver a message. A card that says “Rest” or “Trust the timing” is easy to take in. It's quicker and gentler, which some people prefer first thing in the morning. But it gives you less to work with, and if you draw a card whose message doesn't fit your day, there isn't much else in it.

Which is better for a daily reflection habit?

There's no single right answer, but here's how we'd think about it.

Choose tarot if

  • You want a practice that grows with you. After a year of daily cards you'll know 78 symbols well, and they'll work with any deck you pick up.
  • You like to write. Tarot's open-ended scenes give you more to journal about.
  • You enjoy history and symbolism. Tarot has six centuries of it, and a large body of books written about it.
  • You want to talk about cards with other people. Tarot is a shared vocabulary; oracle decks aren't.

Choose an oracle deck if

  • You want something you can use today, with no learning.
  • You'd rather have a short, direct message than a picture to interpret.
  • A particular deck's art or theme speaks to you. That alone is a good reason.
  • Some tarot imagery, like the Devil or the Ten of Swords, puts you off.

Or use both

Plenty of people draw a tarot card to think about and an oracle card as a one-word theme for the day. They don't conflict. If you do mix them, keep separate notes for each so you can see what each one actually gave you.

Are oracle cards “easier” than tarot?

To start, yes. To get much out of over a long time, not necessarily. Tarot's learning curve is real but gentle if you take it one card a day: you meet every card several times a year in the context of your own life. Our guide to doing a daily tarot pull shows how, and the Major Arcana meanings are a good first 22 to learn. The Minor Arcana follow patterns (each suit has a theme, each number has a rough character), so the remaining 56 come faster than you'd expect.

Questions

What is the main difference between tarot and oracle cards?

Tarot is a fixed 78-card system with 22 Major Arcana and 56 Minor Arcana in four suits, so meanings carry across decks. Oracle decks have no set number of cards or shared meanings; each creator defines their own.

Can I use oracle cards and tarot cards together?

Yes. A common approach is to draw a tarot card to reflect on and an oracle card as a short theme for the day. Keep notes for each separately so you can see what each adds.

Are Lenormand cards oracle cards?

They're often shelved with oracle cards, but Lenormand is its own system: 36 cards with broadly agreed meanings, read in combinations. The deck's imagery comes from a German game, The Game of Hope, published in 1799.

Do I need to learn meanings to use oracle cards?

Usually not. Most oracle decks print a word or message on each card and explain it in the booklet. Tarot cards usually show only a scene and a name, so you learn their meanings over time.

Is Daily Oracle a tarot app or an oracle card app?

A tarot app. It uses the 78-card Rider-Waite-Smith deck from 1909. The name refers to the daily habit of consulting something each morning, not to oracle cards.
