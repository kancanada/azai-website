One on one meeting template for managers

Updated 3 November 2026 · 7 min read
Short answerA good one on one meeting is the employee's meeting, not a status update. Use a short, repeating agenda: their topics first, then progress and blockers, then growth and feedback, then agreed next steps with an owner and a date. Keep a running document you both can see, review last time's commitments at the start, and send the follow-ups the same day.

The one on one meeting is the manager's most valuable half hour, and the easiest to waste. Without a template it drifts into a status update you could have read in a message. This one on one meeting template keeps it about the person: what's on their mind, what's in their way, and what you'll each do next.

What a one on one is for

A 1:1 is time for the person who reports to you. It's where problems surface early, feedback gets said out loud, and trust is built a little each week. Status updates belong in a shared tracker or a team meeting. If you spend the half hour on project updates, you've lost the one meeting where people raise what they wouldn't say in a group.

The one on one meeting agenda

Use the same five parts every time. Familiar structure lets people prepare, and it keeps the meeting from being taken over by whatever is urgent this week.

  1. Last time's commitments (2 minutes). What did each of you promise? Done, or new date. Start with yours.
  2. Their topics (10 to 15 minutes). Whatever they've added to the shared document. This comes first, before your agenda takes the time.
  3. Priorities and blockers (5 minutes). Not a status report. Just: are we working on the right things, and what's in the way that you can remove?
  4. Growth, feedback and well-being (5 minutes). Not every week in depth, but every week for a moment.
  5. Next steps (3 minutes). Read back what each of you will do and by when.

Questions to ask in a one on one

Opening

  • What's on your mind this week?
  • What would make this half hour worth it for you?

Priorities and blockers

  • What's slowing you down that I could help with?
  • Is there anything you're waiting on from me?
  • If you could drop one thing from your plate, what would it be?

Growth

  • What have you learned recently that you want to do more of?
  • What kind of work would you like more of next quarter?
  • Where do you want to be in a year, and what would help you get there?

Feedback, both ways

  • What's one thing I could do differently that would help you?
  • Is there anything I've asked for recently that wasn't clear?
  • Here's something you did well this week, and why it mattered.

Well-being

  • How's your workload, honestly?
  • Is anything outside work making this a hard stretch?

Don't use them all. Pick two or three that fit the week, and let the person's topics lead.

Copy-and-paste one on one template

Keep one running document per person, newest meeting at the top, shared with them so they can add topics in advance.

1:1 — [Name] and [Manager] Date: [date] · Length: [30 min] Last time's commitments - [Commitment] — [Owner] — Done / New date: [date] Their topics - [Topic]: [notes, and what we agreed] Priorities and blockers - Top priorities: [one to three] - Blockers: [what's in the way] — I'll help by: [action] Growth and feedback - Feedback given: [specific, with why it mattered] - Feedback received: [what I'll change] - Growth: [goal or interest, and next step] Well-being - [One line, or "nothing to note"] Next steps - [Action] — Owner: [name] — Due: [date] - [Action] — Owner: [name] — Due: [date] Next 1:1: [date]

How often, and how long

  • Weekly, 30 minutes is a common default for direct reports, especially new or busy ones.
  • Every two weeks, 30 to 45 minutes works for experienced people on steady work.
  • Monthly, 45 to 60 minutes for skip-levels, mentoring or senior people who need little day-to-day support.

Whatever you choose, protect it. Move a 1:1 if you must, but don't cancel it. Repeated cancellations tell people where they rank.

Recording a one on one, respectfully

A recording means you can listen fully instead of typing, and nothing you promised gets lost. But a 1:1 is a personal conversation, so handle it with care.

  • Ask first, every time, and accept "no" without question. Make it easy to say no.
  • Say why. "So I can listen instead of typing, and so I don't forget what I've promised you."
  • Stop when asked. Pause for anything personal, health-related or that the person wants off the record.
  • Share the notes with them afterwards, so the record is shared, not one-sided.
  • Follow the law and your policy. Some US states require everyone's consent to record. See one-party and all-party consent states in the US, and check your organization's rules.
  • Think about where the audio goes. 1:1s often include performance, pay and health. If you record, prefer an app that keeps the recording on your phone. See how to choose a private AI note taker.

How notes and to-dos make 1:1s work

The 1:1 that builds trust is the one where the manager does what they said. Good notes make that easy:

  • Write commitments as action items: a verb, one owner, a date. See how to write action items people actually do.
  • Send a short recap the same day, three or four lines: what we agreed and who does what.
  • Open the next 1:1 with the list. Thirty seconds, item by item.
  • Keep the history. When review season comes, months of 1:1 notes are far better evidence than memory.

Questions

What should be on a one on one meeting agenda?

Their topics first, then a quick check on priorities and blockers, then growth, feedback and well-being, and finally the next steps you both agreed, each with an owner and a date. Start by reviewing what you each promised last time.

How often should managers hold one on ones?

Weekly or every two weeks for direct reports is a common rhythm; monthly can work for experienced people who need little support. Pick a cadence you can keep. A regular 1:1 that's rarely cancelled matters more than its exact length.

How long should a one on one be?

Thirty minutes suits most weekly 1:1s. Book 45 to 60 minutes for a monthly 1:1, or when a career or performance conversation is planned.

Should I record one on one meetings?

Only with the other person's clear agreement, and never for a sensitive conversation they'd rather keep off the record. Follow consent law where you are and your organization's policy, and offer to share the notes.

Who should take notes in a one on one?

Both of you can, in one shared document. The manager should always capture their own commitments, because those are the ones most often forgotten.
