Most meeting notes fail in one of two ways: a page of half-sentences nobody can read a week later, or a near-transcript that buries the one decision that mattered. Learning how to take meeting notes is mostly learning what not to write. The aim is simple: anyone should be able to see what was decided and who's doing what in under a minute.

What to capture in meeting notes

  • Decisions, written as plain sentences: "We'll launch on 18 November."
  • Action items, each with one owner and a due date.
  • Open questions, and who will answer them.
  • Facts you'll need later: numbers, dates, names, prices, commitments made to clients.
  • Who was there, if it matters later who heard what.

What to skip

  • The discussion that led to a decision, unless the reasoning will be questioned.
  • Who said what in passing.
  • Anything already in the slides or the pre-read. Write "see slide 6" instead.

Three note-taking methods for meetings

1. The outline

One heading per agenda item, with short bullets underneath. It's the fastest method and works for most team meetings, because the agenda gives you the structure before the meeting starts. Write the headings in advance and leave space under each.

2. Decisions, actions, questions

Split the page into three labeled sections, or prefix each line with a letter: D for decision, A for action, Q for open question. Everything else is optional. This is the best method for busy managers, because the notes are already sorted into what you need to send afterwards.

[Meeting] · [date] · [who was there] Decisions D [What was agreed, as a sentence] Actions A [Verb + result] — [owner] — [due date] Open questions Q [Question] — [who will answer] — [by when] Notes - [Numbers, names, dates worth keeping]

3. The Cornell method

Developed by Cornell education professor Walter Pauk, it divides the page into a wide notes column, a narrow column for cue words or questions, and a short summary at the bottom. You write notes during the meeting, then add cues and the summary afterwards. It's more than a quick stand-up needs, but it works well for long briefings, training and board packs you'll need to revisit.

How to take meeting notes during the meeting

  • Write in your own words. Summarizing makes you think about what's being said; copying it word for word doesn't.
  • Capture actions the moment they're said. When someone says "we should", ask "who, and by when?"
  • Mark what matters. A star or a letter in the margin beats rewriting later.
  • Read the actions back in the last two minutes, so owners agree before they leave.

The five minutes after the meeting

This is where notes become useful. Do it straight away, while you still remember what your shorthand meant.

  1. Fill the gaps: names, numbers and anything half-written.
  2. Pull out decisions and actions to the top.
  3. Turn actions into tasks with a verb, one owner and a date. See how to write action items people actually do.
  4. Send a short recap the same day. For formal meetings, use the meeting minutes template.
  5. File the raw notes where you'll find them, named by meeting and date.

Handwriting vs typing vs recording

MethodGood forWatch out for
HandwritingStaying present; no screen between you and the roomHard to search or share; you rewrite actions afterwards
TypingSpeed, search, sending notes straight awayDrifting into word-for-word transcribing; looks like email to others
RecordingFull attention on the conversation; an exact record of numbers and promisesNeeds everyone's consent; you still need decisions and actions, not just a transcript

You may have heard that handwriting beats typing. A widely cited 2014 study by Mueller and Oppenheimer found students who took notes by hand did better on conceptual questions than those on laptops, and linked this to laptop users copying lectures word for word. Later direct replications, by Morehead and colleagues (2019) and Urry and colleagues (2021), did not consistently find the advantage. The practical lesson that holds up: whatever you use, summarize instead of transcribing.

Recording takes the transcribing off your hands altogether, which is useful when you're chairing. Tell people first and follow the consent rules where you are: see recording consent laws in the US.
