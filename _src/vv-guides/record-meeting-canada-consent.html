Recording a meeting is the easiest way to get accurate notes. Before you press Record, it helps to know the two sets of rules that apply in Canada: the criminal law about intercepting conversations, and the privacy laws about collecting people's personal information. They answer different questions, and both can matter.

The short version

  • You're in the conversation: recording it is generally not a crime, even if you don't tell anyone.
  • You're not in the conversation: recording it without any participant's agreement can be a criminal offence.
  • A business is recording clients or staff: privacy law generally means telling people, explaining why, and keeping the recording safe.
  • Your workplace has a policy: it can forbid recording even where the law allows it.
  • Someone on the call is in the US: some states require everyone's consent. See recording a meeting in the US.

The Criminal Code: one-party consent

Section 184 of the Criminal Code makes it an offence to intercept a "private communication" with a device, punishable by up to five years in prison. A private communication is, roughly, one where the people talking could reasonably expect that only the intended listeners would hear it. A closed-door meeting usually fits.

The key exception: it is not an offence if the person recording has the consent, express or implied, of someone in the conversation. When you are in the meeting yourself, your own consent counts. That is why Canada is described as a one-party consent country.

Two things follow:

  1. Don't record conversations you're not part of. Leaving your phone recording in a meeting room after you walk out, or recording a conversation between other people, is where the criminal risk is.
  2. An illegal recording is a problem to use, too. Section 193 makes it an offence, with some exceptions, to use or disclose a private communication that was intercepted without consent.

Privacy law: when a business records

A voice recording is personal information. When an organization records people as part of its business, privacy law applies on top of the Criminal Code:

  • PIPEDA, the federal law, covers most private-sector organizations in commercial activity, and employees of federally regulated businesses such as banks, airlines and telecom companies.
  • British Columbia, Alberta and Quebec have their own private-sector privacy laws that apply instead, for businesses operating within those provinces. BC and Alberta's laws also cover employee information.

The federal Privacy Commissioner's guidance on recording customer calls is a practical model for any recorded conversation. Tell people at the outset that you're recording. Explain the purpose as clearly and narrowly as you can. Offer an alternative if they'd rather not be recorded. Use the recording only for that purpose, keep it secure, and don't keep it longer than you need. If you use a contractor or service to record or transcribe, you remain responsible for how it handles the recording.

In October 2026, the Commissioner updated that guidance with a focus on voiceprints: the unique characteristics of a person's voice, used to recognize them. It calls voiceprints sensitive biometric information, which may need people's express consent.

Recording at work

Employers recording staff meetings generally need to give notice and have a reasonable purpose, and collective agreements may add rules. Employees recording their own meetings are usually not committing a crime, but a secret recording can still break a workplace policy and cause problems at work. If a meeting matters enough to record, it usually works better to say so.

Quebec

The Criminal Code applies in Quebec as everywhere else. Quebec's Civil Code also protects privacy and lists intercepting or using someone's private communications as an invasion of privacy, and Quebec has its own private-sector privacy law. Quebec courts have generally allowed people to use recordings of conversations they took part in as evidence, but they have excluded recordings in some cases.

AI note takers and consent

AI note takers raise the same questions, plus two more:

  • Where does the recording go? Cloud services upload the audio to their servers, often outside Canada, to transcribe and summarize it. For a business, that is personal information handled by a service provider, and you stay accountable for it.
  • Does it build voiceprints? Some tools learn people's voices so they can recognize them in future meetings. Given the Commissioner's view of voiceprints as sensitive biometric information, check what your tool does and get consent where needed.

A simple checklist before you record

  1. Say it at the start: "I'm recording this so I can write up accurate notes. Is everyone OK with that?"
  2. Put it in the invite for regular or external meetings.
  3. Offer to stop for sensitive parts, and do it.
  4. Stop when you leave the room.
  5. Keep it safe: a locked phone, and backups only where your organization allows.
  6. Share the summary, not the audio, unless people need the full record.
  7. Delete it when you no longer need it.
